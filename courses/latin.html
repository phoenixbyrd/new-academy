Tongues I

Latin I

Learn to read Latin — the language of Rome, of law, and of science.

1

Sound: the alphabet, classical pronunciation, and stress

▾

Objectives

  • Pronounce every Latin letter the Roman way — including v as /w/ and c always as /k/.
  • Hear and produce the difference between long and short vowels.
  • Apply the Latin stress rule without thinking: never the last syllable; penult if heavy, otherwise antepenult.

Two pronunciations, one right choice for us

Latin uses the alphabet you're reading now — because this alphabet is the Latin alphabet, borrowed by English. Classical Latin had 23 letters: no j (written i), and u and v were one letter (v). The letters k, y, z appear only in borrowed Greek words.

Two pronunciations of Latin survive today. The restored classical pronunciation reconstructs how educated Romans spoke around Cicero's time, using evidence: Roman poetry's meter only scans if vowel length is right, Greek writers transliterated Caesar as Kaisar (proving the hard c), and ancient grammarians described the sounds explicitly. The ecclesiastical pronunciation is the Italianate church Latin of the Middle Ages — c before e/i becomes "ch," v becomes "v." We use the restored pronunciation because we are reading Cicero, not chanting the Mass — and every rule below is a classical rule.

Consonants the Roman way

Pronounce it the Roman way (the "restored" classical pronunciation), not the church way:

  • c is always hard, like k: Cicerō is "KI-ke-roh," never "SI-se-roh."
  • g is always hard: ager (field) is "AH-ger."
  • v is w: veni is "WEH-nee." Hence Veni, vidi, vici = "WEH-nee WEE-dee WEE-kee."
  • i before another vowel is y: iam (now) is "yam."
  • ae sounds like eye: Caesar is "KAI-sar." au is ow (as in "how"): aurum (gold) is "OW-rum." oe is oy: poena (penalty) is "POY-nah."
  • qu is kw; gn is ng-n: regnum (kingdom) is "RENG-num."

Two fine points. An s between vowels stays voiceless — rosa is "ROH-sah," not "ROH-zah" the way English would voice it. And x is always ks: lēx (law) is "lakes." Drill these until v-as-w feels automatic; it is the single sound that marks you as reading classically.

Vowel length changes meaning

Vowels come in long and short pairs, marked with a macron (ā) when long. Length matters: malum (apple) vs. mālum (evil) differ only in the first vowel's length.

English has the same distinction hidden in spelling: "bit" vs. "beat," "full" vs. "fool." Latin just writes it with one vowel and lets length do the work. More minimal pairs to train your ear: annus (year, "AHN-nus") vs. ānus (old woman, "AHH-nus" — a very different word); liber (book) vs. līber (a free man); venit (he comes) vs. vēnit (he came) — one long vowel flips present to past. Macrons in this course are training wheels: dictionaries mark them, real Roman texts don't, so learn to expect length where the grammar demands it.

Stress: the rule that never breaks

Stress rule (memorize this — it never breaks):

  1. Never stress the last syllable.
  2. Stress the second-to-last syllable (the penult) if it's long — long by nature (a macron, like amāre → a-MAH-reh) or long by position (its vowel is followed by two consonants, like puella → pu-EL-lah, because of the double ll).
  3. Otherwise stress the third-to-last syllable (the antepenult): agricola → a-GRI-ko-lah.

Two-syllable words are easy: the penult is also the first syllable, so just stress it — ROH-ma, WEH-nee. And note what "long by position" really means: the syllable is heavy because its vowel is closed by a consonant cluster (pu-el-la). A double consonant always does it; so does any pair like -st- or -ct-. Once you can scan a word's syllables, you can stress any Latin word you've never seen.

A primary source in three words

The biographer Suetonius (Life of Julius Caesar 37) reports that in Caesar's triumph after the lightning campaign in Pontus, among the floats of the procession was carried a placard with just three words: Veni, vidi, vici — "I came, I saw, I conquered." In restored pronunciation it crackles: "WEH-nee WEE-dee WEE-kee," three hammer blows of hard c and short vowels. Say it until you can rattle it off — it is your first fluent Latin sentence, and every sound in it follows the rules of this lesson.

Try
  1. Read these aloud with classical pronunciation and mark the stress: Caesar, veni, aurum, poena, regnum, amāre, puella, agricola, Rōmānus, fēmina.
  2. Write out veni, vidi, vici in plain English phonetics ("WEH-nee WEE-dee WEE-kee") and say it until it feels natural.
  3. Decide the stress of Rōmānus and fēmina by the rule before checking: the penult of Rōmānus has a long ā, so roh-MAH-nus; the penult of fēmina is short, so the stress falls back — FEH-mi-na.
  4. Say malum vs. mālum and annus vs. ānus aloud, exaggerating the vowel length until you can hear the difference.
Check
How did Cicero pronounce his own name, and why is "SIS-e-roh" wrong?
"KI-ke-roh." Classical Latin has no soft c — c is always hard, and i before a vowel glides to y-like. The "s" sound for c developed centuries later.
Check
Why is the stress pu-EL-la and not PU-el-la?
The penult el is heavy by position — the double ll closes the syllable. Heavy penult gets the stress. (PU-el-la would violate the rule only if the penult were light, which it isn't.)
Check
A student reads "Caesar" as "SEE-zer." Name two things wrong.
First, c is always hard: K, never S. Second, ae is the diphthong "eye": KAI-sar. (The stress is right by luck — KAI-sar — since the penult is heavy.)

Summary

Classical Latin has 23 letters, hard c and g, v as /w/, and diphthongs ae ("eye"), au ("ow"), oe ("oy"). Vowel length is meaningful — malum vs. mālum — and stress follows the iron rule: never the last syllable, penult if heavy, otherwise antepenult. Read veni, vidi, vici aloud and you are already speaking like Suetonius's Caesar.

2

Nouns: the 1st and 2nd declensions, nominative and accusative

▾

Objectives

  • Parse nominative and accusative endings in real sentences.
  • Decline any 1st- or 2nd-declension noun through all five cases, singular and plural.
  • Use the neuter rule: nominative and accusative are always identical for neuter nouns.

Case: the noun's job title

A Latin noun changes its ending to show its case — its job in the sentence. Two cases do most of the work:

  • Nominative: the subject (who acts). Puella — "the girl" (as subject).
  • Accusative: the direct object (who is acted upon). Puellam — "the girl" (as object).

Because the endings carry the roles, word order is free: Puella servum videt and Servum puella videt both mean "The girl sees the slave." Grammar's primer introduced this idea — now you learn the actual machinery.

Three more cases complete the toolkit, and you'll use them from lesson 4: the genitive ("of the girl"), the dative ("to/for the girl"), and the ablative ("by/with/from the girl"). (A sixth, the vocative for direct address — "O girl!" — mostly copies the nominative.) Learn the full tables now and the later lessons get easier, not harder.

The first declension, complete

1st declension (mostly feminine; the -a family). The full endings, nominative to ablative:

SingularPlural
Nominativepuellapuellae
Genitivepuellae — of the girlpuellārum — of the girls
Dativepuellae — to/for the girlpuellīs — to/for the girls
Accusativepuellampuellās
Ablativepuellā — by/with/from the girlpuellīs — by/with/from the girls

Notice the overlaps: genitive and dative singular are both -ae — context tells them apart. The giveaway ending is -ārum: nothing else in Latin looks like a 1st-declension genitive plural. And remember the rule-breakers: poēta (poet) and agricola (farmer) decline exactly like puella but are masculine — poētae (the poets), agricolās (the farmers, accusative). Dictionary entries give the genitive so you know the declension: puella, -ae means 1st declension, full stop.

2nd declension (masculine -us, neuter -um):

Notice: neuter nouns always have identical nominative and accusative (bellum, bella). That's a rule for all neuter nouns in every declension.

The -ōrum ending is the 2nd-declension twin of -ārum — both unmistakable genitive plurals. One more detail for completeness: the vocative singular of -us nouns ends in -e — serve! ("slave!"), domine! ("master!"). You'll meet it in dialogue and inscriptions.

Reading real sentences

Parse before you translate — name each noun's case and job. In Servus dominum timet: servus is nominative singular (the subject, the one fearing), dominum is accusative singular (the object, the one feared). So: "The slave fears the master." Now flip it: Dominus servum timet — "The master fears the slave." Same words, different endings, opposite meaning. That's the whole game of lesson 2: endings, not order, decide who does what.

A primary source: Virgil's first line

Virgil's Aeneid (19 BC) opens with the most famous line in Latin poetry: Arma virumque canō. Gloss: arma — weapons (accusative plural, neuter — identical to the nominative, per the rule above); virum — the man (accusative singular); -que — "and" (tacked onto the end of the word it joins); canō — I sing (the -ō ending is the pronoun "I," as lesson 3 will prove). Translation: "Arms and the man I sing." With three endings you can already parse the opening of Rome's national epic.

New nouns to memorize: servus (slave), dominus (master), amīcus (friend), inimīcus (enemy), poēta (poet — 1st declension but masculine), agricola (farmer — also 1st declension, masculine), fīlia (daughter), rēgīna (queen), bellum (war), verbum (word).

Try
  1. Translate, watching the endings: Dominus servōs laudat. Poētae fīliās amant. Rēgīna bellum timet. (timet = fears)
  2. Write out the full declension of rēgīna from memory — all five cases, singular and plural — then check it against the puella table.
  3. Parse every noun in Amīcus dominum in forō videt: give each one's case and its job in the sentence, then translate.
  4. English to Latin: "The queen fears war." "The slaves love the poet's daughter." (Use timet, amant; the poet's daughter = poētae fīliam.)
Check
In Puella servum videt, who sees and who is seen — and how do you know?
The girl sees; the slave is seen. Puella is nominative (subject), servum is accusative (object). The endings decide, regardless of word order.
Check
Why can bellum be either the subject or the object of a sentence?
The neuter rule: nominative and accusative are identical for neuter nouns. Bellum Rōmam dēlet ("war destroys Rome") has bellum as subject; Rōma bellum timet ("Rome fears war") has it as object. Same form — the verb and context disambiguate.
Check
Parse poētārum: case, number, and meaning.
Genitive plural, 1st declension: "of the poets." The -ārum ending is the unmistakable marker — and yes, poēta is masculine, but it still declines in the 1st declension.

Summary

Case endings assign every noun its job: nominative acts, accusative is acted upon, genitive owns, dative receives, ablative accompanies. The 1st declension (puella, -ae) and 2nd (servus, -ī; bellum, -ī) cover a huge share of the Latin you'll read, and the neuter nom=acc rule means bellum never changes between subject and object. Parse the endings first; the translation follows.

3

Verbs: the present tense, 1st conjugation, and esse

▾

Objectives

  • Chant the six present personal endings (-ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt) forward and backward.
  • Conjugate any 1st-conjugation verb from its stem.
  • Know sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt cold.

The ending is the pronoun

Latin verbs don't need pronouns: the ending is the pronoun. The 1st conjugation (the -āre family, e.g. amāre, to love) conjugates like this in the present:

PersonSingularPlural
1st (I / we)amō — I loveamāmus — we love
2nd (you)amās — you loveamātis — you (all) love
3rd (he, she, it / they)amat — he/she lovesamant — they love

Learn the six endings as a chant: -ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. They attach to nearly every Latin verb you'll meet: vocō (I call), laudat (he praises), labōrant (they work), pugnāmus (we fight).

How the stem works

Find the stem by dropping -re from the infinitive: amā-re → stem amā-. The endings bolt straight onto it. Watch the hidden quantity, though: the stem vowel is short before -ō, -s, -t, -nt (amŏ, amăs, amat, amant) and long before -mus, -tis (amāmus, amātis). The macron you see in amāre only shows itself in half the forms. Real Latin texts don't print macrons, but poetry's meter depends on this exact alternation — learn to feel where the long vowel hides.

Esse: the verb you must know cold

The one verb you must know cold is esse, to be — it's irregular, and it's everywhere:

SingularPlural
1stsum — I amsumus — we are
2ndes — you areestis — you (all) are
3rdest — he/she/it issunt — they are

Esse is the copula — the equals sign of Latin: Rōma magna est ("Rome is great") puts both sides in the nominative. You'll meet it in the capstone's very first sentence, Gallia est omnis dīvīsa. Drill it as its own chant — sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt — until it's faster than your name.

Your endings already read other verbs

Latin has four conjugations, but the six personal endings never change. What changes is the stem vowel: vidēre (2nd) gives videt (he sees), dīcere (3rd) gives dīcit (he says), audīre (4th) gives audit (he hears). The -t always means "he/she/it," the -nt always "they." So even before you've studied the other conjugations, you can already read venit (he comes), dīcunt (they say), and canit (he/she sings) — the ending tells you the person, and the stem tells you the action.

A primary source: two words from Catullus

Catullus's Poem 85 is the shortest great poem in Latin, and its first line is pure present tense: Ōdī et amō. Gloss: ōdī — I hate (a perfect-tense form with present meaning: "I have conceived a hatred," i.e., I hate); et — and; amō — I love. The poem continues: quārē id faciam, fortasse requīris? nesciō, sed fierī sentiō et excrucior — "Why I do this, perhaps you ask? I don't know, but I feel it happening, and I am torn apart." Written around 55 BC about his torment over Lesbia, it shows the present tense doing emotional work: amō is not "to love" in the abstract — it's I love, right now, helplessly.

New verbs to memorize: vocō (call), laudō (praise), pugnō (fight), labōrō (work), ambulō (walk), portō (carry).

Try
  1. Conjugate laudō (I praise) fully, aloud, then translate: Servī dominum laudant. Poēta ambulat et canit. (canit = sings — 3rd conjugation, but the -t ending still tells you "he/she sings")
  2. Chant the six endings backwards — -nt, -tis, -mus, -t, -s, -ō — until it's instant, then forwards again.
  3. Translate: Vidēmus et audīmus. Es Rōmānus? (Watch the -mus and the es.)
  4. Find and label every form of sum in: Rōma magna est. Poētae in forō sunt. Sum amīcus tuus.
Check
Why does Amāmus Rōmam need no word for "we"?
The -mus ending already means "we" — the subject is built into the verb. ("We love Rome.") Latin adds explicit pronouns like nōs only for emphasis or contrast.
Check
Give the 2nd person plural of portō and translate it.
Portātis — "you (all) carry." Stem portā- (long before -tis) plus the 2nd-plural ending -tis.
Check
Amat ends in -t for "he loves," but est doesn't look like it follows the pattern. Why?
Esse is irregular — it has no regular stem + ending structure. You can't derive sum, es, est from the 1st-conjugation pattern; that's why it's memorized as its own chant: sum, es, est, sumus, estis, sunt.

Summary

Six endings carry every person and number: -ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. Strip -re off the infinitive to find the stem (amā-), bolt on the ending, and watch the stem vowel go short before -ō/-s/-t/-nt and long before -mus/-tis. Esse breaks the pattern and must be memorized whole. With this lesson you can already conjugate amō — and read Catullus's Ōdī et amō.

4

Building sentences: word order, adjectives, prepositions

▾

Objectives

  • Read Latin's default subject–object–verb order and spot emphasis by position.
  • Make adjectives agree with their nouns in gender, number, and case.
  • Pair each preposition with the case it demands — especially in + ablative vs. in + accusative.

SOV: the Roman default

Latin's default word order is subject – object – verb: Puella servum vocat ("The girl the slave calls"). The verb goes last; the first and last positions are the emphatic ones, so Romans moved words there for stress. Case endings keep the meaning exact no matter the order.

Read SOV as a rhythm: setup, setup, punchline. Caesar in Galliam vēnit — "Caesar, into Gaul, came." The verb lands last like a drumbeat. When a Roman breaks the pattern — Vēnit Caesar — the fronted verb shouts: "Came, Caesar (did)!" Position is emphasis; endings are grammar. Never confuse the two.

Adjectives agree three ways at once

Adjectives agree with the noun they describe in gender, number, and case — three things at once. Bonus (good) is a 1st/2nd-declension adjective:

MasculineFeminineNeuter
Nom. sg.bonusbonabonum
Acc. sg.bonumbonambonum
Nom. pl.bonībonaebona
Acc. pl.bonōsbonāsbona

So: puella bona (a good girl), servus bonus (a good slave), bellum bonum (a good war — well, a successful one). And in a sentence: Puella bona servum malum vocat — "The good girl calls the bad slave." The adjective's ending, not its position, tells you which noun it belongs to.

The masculine column is 2nd declension, the feminine is 1st, the neuter is 2nd neuter — you've already learned all three machines in lesson 2. Magnus, malus, parvus, novus, multus all work exactly like bonus. Adjectives usually follow their noun (puella bona) but can precede for emphasis (bona puella — "a good girl"). Either way, only agreement binds them.

The ablative: the case you've been using all along

Look back at the examples: in forō, cum amīcīs, ab urbe, sine metū — every one is an ablative. The ablative is the "everything else" case: location (in), accompaniment (with), separation (from), and more. Its endings are already in your tables: 1st declension -ā singular (puellā), -īs plural (puellīs); 2nd declension -ō singular (servō), -īs plural (servīs). The master rule of thumb: accusative prepositions mean motion (ad, in + acc, per — toward, into, through), ablative prepositions mean rest or separation (in + abl, ab, cum, sine — in, from, with).

A primary source: two words of farewell

Catullus's Poem 101, an elegy for his brother buried at Troy, ends with the most quoted farewell in Latin: ave atque valē. Gloss: avē — hail! (imperative of avēre, "to be well"); atque — and; valē — farewell! (imperative of valēre, "to be strong/well"). Translation: "Hail and farewell." Two imperatives, one greeting the dead and one releasing him — grammar lesson aside, Romans said valē the way we say "goodbye" (which is itself a contraction of "God be with ye"). Two thousand years later, "ave atque vale" still closes eulogies.

New adjectives: malus (bad), magnus (great, big), parvus (small), novus (new), multus (much, many).

Try
  1. Translate: Magna puella parvum servum in forō videt. Amīcī cum poētā ad urbem ambulant. Then rearrange the first sentence's words and confirm the meaning doesn't change.
  2. Make magnus agree with each noun phrase: rēgīna (accusative singular) → magnam; bella (accusative plural) → magna; servus (nominative singular) → magnus. Then use each in a sentence.
  3. Translate: Cum amīcīs in urbe ambulāmus. Post bellum pāx est. (Name the case after each preposition as you go.)
  4. Take Puella bona servum malum vocat and move vocat to the front. How does the emphasis change? Confirm the meaning stays identical.
Check
In forō vs. in forum — what's the difference?
Case. In with the ablative means "in, on" (location — standing in the forum); in with the accusative means "into" (motion — walking into the forum). The preposition is the same; the case carries the distinction.
Check
In puella bona, what proves bona describes puella and not some other noun?
Agreement. Bona is feminine nominative singular — it matches puella in gender, number, and case. A masculine accusative noun like servum could only take bonum. The ending binds adjective to noun, wherever each stands.
Check
Ad urbem vs. ab urbe — which is motion toward the city, and how do you tell?
Ad urbem — ad takes the accusative, and accusative prepositions mean motion: "to/toward the city." Ab urbe is ab + ablative: motion away — "away from the city."

Summary

Latin defaults to subject–object–verb with the verb landing last, and Romans spend the first and last slots on emphasis. Adjectives shadow their nouns in gender, number, and case — bonus runs all three declension machines at once. Prepositions demand a case, and the case decides the meaning: accusative for motion (ad urbem), ablative for rest or separation (in forō, ab urbe). With these tools you can build — and unscramble — real Latin sentences.

Core vocabulary: the 60 words that unlock Latin — and Latin alive in English

▾

Objectives

  • Learn the 60 highest-frequency words — nouns, verbs, adjectives, and small words.
  • Use opposites and English echoes to memorize faster.
  • Decode real Latin phrases (law, science, mottos) unaided.

How to learn 60 words fast

Don't memorize a list — memorize pairs. Opposites lock each other in: bonus/malus (good/bad), amīcus/inimīcus (friend/enemy — note in- negates, as in English), vīta/mors (life/death), pāx/bellum (peace/war), novus/antīquus (new/old), parvus/magnus (small/great), post/ante (after/before). Learn the small words as a toolkit, not vocabulary: et, sed, non, in, ad, cum, sine are the joints of every sentence you'll ever read.

Use the English echoes as grappling hooks: videō → video ("I see" — your camera app is a Latin sentence), audiō → audio, scrībō → scribe, dīcō → dictate, faciō → factory (a place where things are made), tempus → temporal, mors → mortal, verbum → verbal. Every echo is a memory hook already installed in your head.

Latin alive in English

Latin alive in English. You speak fragments of Latin daily:

  • Law: habeas corpus ("you shall have the body" — the right against unlawful detention), subpoena (sub + poena, "under penalty"), pro bono ("for the good" — free legal work), versus ("turned against").
  • Science: Homo sapiens ("wise man"), terra firma ("solid earth"), and every species name ever published.
  • Mottos: Harvard's Veritas ("truth"), tempus fugit ("time flies"), caveat emptor ("let the buyer beware"), alma mater ("nourishing mother" — your school).
  • Everyday words: exit (ex + it, "he/she goes out" — the sign above the door is a Latin verb), agenda ("things to be done"), alibi ("elsewhere"), alias ("at another time" — the courtroom term for "otherwise known as").

A primary source: Cicero's outburst

In 63 BC, Cicero rose in the Senate to denounce Catiline's conspiracy and opened his attack with a cry of moral shock: Ō tempora, ō mōrēs! (In Catilinam 1.2). Gloss: ō — oh!; tempora — the times (accusative plural, neuter — the exclamatory accusative, "oh, these times!"); mōrēs — customs, morals (accusative plural). Translation: "Oh, the times! Oh, the customs!" — as in, "look what the age has come to." Every word is in your 60 (the ō is just the interjection), and the phrase still headlines editorials 2,089 years later.

Try
  1. Drill the 60 with the flashcards on the Review page, nouns and verbs first. Then decode three real phrases from the lists above without help: pro bono publico, terra incognita, magna cum laude.
  2. Pair the opposites from the lists without looking — bonus with…? vīta with…? post with…? — then check.
  3. Guess the English from the Latin root: audible (audiō), temporal (tempus), mortal (mors), verbal (verbum). Say each root aloud as you do.
  4. Write five simple Latin sentences using only the 60 words — e.g. Amīcus meus ad urbem ambulat ("My friend walks to the city"). Trade with a partner or check your own endings.

Check
If habeō means "I have," what does habeas mean in habeas corpus, and what is the phrase demanding?
"You (singular) shall have" — a command to the jailer. The writ orders whoever holds a prisoner to produce the body in court: it's a demand for the person, a shield against detention without trial.
Check
Your phone's camera app says "video." Why is that a complete Latin sentence?
It's videō, 1st person singular present: "I see." The -ō ending is the pronoun — the app is literally declaring "I see" every time you open it.

Summary

Sixty words — learned as opposite pairs and English echoes, not as a list — unlock the bulk of the Latin you'll ever read. The same roots run the courtroom (subpoena, habeas corpus), the laboratory (Homo sapiens), and the signs over the doors (exit). You already speak Latin daily; now you can parse it.

Capstone: Caesar's Gaul and a real Roman inscription

▾

Objectives

  • Parse every word of the most famous first sentence in Latin prose.
  • Read a simplified Caesar passage and a real Roman inscription unaided.
  • Read both aloud with restored classical pronunciation and correct stress.

The sentence that conquered a genre

Two translations. Use everything from lessons 1–5; every word you haven't met is glossed.

1. Caesar, simplified. Julius Caesar opened his account of the Gallic Wars with the most famous first sentence in Latin prose: Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs — "Gaul as a whole is divided into three parts." Parse it word by word, using every lesson at once:

WordParseMeans
Gallianominative singular, feminine — the subject (lesson 2)Gaul
est3rd singular of esse — "is" (lesson 3)is
omnisnominative singular, feminine — agrees with Gallia (lesson 4)whole, entire
dīvīsaperfect passive participle, nominative singular feminine — agrees with Galliadivided
inpreposition + accusative — motion "into" (lesson 4)into
partēsaccusative plural (3rd declension — new, but the -ēs is unmistakable)parts
trēsaccusative plural — agrees with partēsthree

Notice how much grammar one sentence carries: omnis and dīvīsa are both feminine singular because Gallia is (adjective agreement, lesson 4); trēs matches partēs in case and number; in takes the accusative because division into parts is motion (lesson 4). The sentence goes on to name the three peoples — the Belgae, the Aquītānī, the Celtae — and Caesar's next line is just as readable: Hōrum omnium fortissimī sunt Belgae — "Of all these, the bravest are the Belgae" (hōrum = of these, omnium = of all, fortissimī = bravest, sunt = are).

Context: Caesar wrote De Bello Gallico in 58 BC as both military dispatch and political advertisement, published in installments to keep his name before the Roman public while he conquered Gaul. That first sentence is pure propaganda craft — cool, totalizing, and grammatically airtight.

Here's a simplified passage in his style:

Caesar in Galliam vēnit. Gallia est terra magna. In Galliā multī populī habitant. Gallī fortiter pugnant. Caesar cum legiōnibus Rōmānīs in Galliā pugnat. Tandem Rōmānī Gallōs vincunt.

Gloss: Gallia = Gaul (modern France); vēnit = came; terra = land; habitant = they live, dwell; fortiter = bravely; legiō = legion (a Roman army unit); tandem = finally, at last; vincunt = they conquer, defeat; Gallōs = the Gauls (accusative plural).

A real inscription

2. A real inscription. This is carved on the Pantheon in Rome, built under Augustus and rebuilt under Hadrian — you can go read it today:

M·AGRIPPA·L·F·COS·III·FECIT

Gloss: M. = Marcus (a first name); AGRIPPA = Agrippa (his family name); L·F = Lucī fīlius = "son of Lucius" (Romans recorded their father's name); COS = consul (the highest Roman office); III = three times; FECIT = he built, he made. The raised dots separate words — Roman inscriptions had no spaces as we know them.

Read it like a Roman official would. Marcus Agrippa shows the tria nomina — the three-name system (first name, clan name, family name) that marked a full citizen. L·F, the filiation, distinguished him from every other Marcus Agrippa by naming his father Lucius. COS III is his résumé: consul three times, the highest office in Rome, held thrice. And FECIT — "he built it" — is the standard builder's signature. One irony: the Pantheon standing today was rebuilt by Hadrian around AD 125, but he kept Agrippa's inscription — Roman piety toward the founder outweighed the architect's ego.

Reading like a Roman

Put lesson 1 to work on both texts. Inscriptions are carved in capitals with V for U (AGRIPPA would be pronounced "ah-GRIP-pah"), and the raised dots (interpuncts) mark word boundaries. Read the Caesar sentence aloud with restored pronunciation: GAL-li-a est OM-nis di-WEE-sa in PAR-tes TRES — hard c's nowhere needed here, but the v in dīvīsa is /w/, and the stress never lands on the final syllable. If you can read these two texts aloud, accurately and at speed, you have finished Latin I.

Why is dīvīsa feminine singular in Gallia est omnis dīvīsa?
Dīvīsa is a participle functioning as an adjective, so it agrees with the noun it describes — Gallia, nominative singular feminine. "Gaul, as a whole, is divided (feminine, like Gaul herself)."
Check
What does L·F stand for, and why did Romans carve it on public buildings?
Lūcī fīlius — "son of Lucius." The filiation identified exactly which Marcus Agrippa built the temple, since Roman men constantly shared names. It was the ancient equivalent of a middle name plus "Jr." — identity carved in stone.

Summary

Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs packs all five lessons into seven words: noun cases, esse, adjective agreement, and in + accusative for motion. The Pantheon inscription shows the same language doing public work — names, offices, and a builder's boast in stone. Read both aloud, parse every ending, and Latin I is complete: you can now open Caesar and Virgil and know what you're looking at.
