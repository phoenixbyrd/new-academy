Tongues I

Latin I

Learn to read Latin — the language of Rome, of law, and of science.

1

Sound: the alphabet, classical pronunciation, and stress

▾

Latin uses the alphabet you're reading now — because this alphabet is the Latin alphabet, borrowed by English. Classical Latin had 23 letters: no j (written i), and u and v were one letter (v). The letters k, y, z appear only in borrowed Greek words.

Pronounce it the Roman way (the "restored" classical pronunciation), not the church way:

  • c is always hard, like k: Cicerō is "KI-ke-roh," never "SI-se-roh."
  • g is always hard: ager (field) is "AH-ger."
  • v is w: veni is "WEH-nee." Hence Veni, vidi, vici = "WEH-nee WEE-dee WEE-kee."
  • i before another vowel is y: iam (now) is "yam."
  • ae sounds like eye: Caesar is "KAI-sar." au is ow (as in "how"): aurum (gold) is "OW-rum." oe is oy: poena (penalty) is "POY-nah."
  • qu is kw; gn is ng-n: regnum (kingdom) is "RENG-num."

Vowels come in long and short pairs, marked with a macron (ā) when long. Length matters: malum (apple) vs. mālum (evil) differ only in the first vowel's length.

Stress rule (memorize this — it never breaks):

  1. Never stress the last syllable.
  2. Stress the second-to-last syllable (the penult) if it's long — long by nature (a macron, like amāre → a-MAH-reh) or long by position (its vowel is followed by two consonants, like puella → pu-EL-lah, because of the double ll).
  3. Otherwise stress the third-to-last syllable (the antepenult): agricola → a-GRI-ko-lah.
Try
Read these aloud with classical pronunciation and mark the stress: Caesar, veni, aurum, poena, regnum, amāre, puella, agricola, Rōmānus, fēmina.
Check
How did Cicero pronounce his own name, and why is "SIS-e-roh" wrong?
"KI-ke-roh." Classical Latin has no soft c — c is always hard, and i before a vowel glides to y-like. The "s" sound for c developed centuries later.
2

Nouns: the 1st and 2nd declensions, nominative and accusative

▾

A Latin noun changes its ending to show its case — its job in the sentence. Two cases do most of the work:

  • Nominative: the subject (who acts). Puella — "the girl" (as subject).
  • Accusative: the direct object (who is acted upon). Puellam — "the girl" (as object).

Because the endings carry the roles, word order is free: Puella servum videt and Servum puella videt both mean "The girl sees the slave." Grammar's primer introduced this idea — now you learn the actual machinery.

Notice: neuter nouns always have identical nominative and accusative (bellum, bella). That's a rule for all neuter nouns in every declension.

New nouns to memorize: servus (slave), dominus (master), amīcus (friend), inimīcus (enemy), poēta (poet — 1st declension but masculine), agricola (farmer — also 1st declension, masculine), fīlia (daughter), rēgīna (queen), bellum (war), verbum (word).

Try
Translate, watching the endings: Dominus servōs laudat. Poētae fīliās amant. Rēgīna bellum timet. (timet = fears)
Check
In Puella servum videt, who sees and who is seen — and how do you know?
The girl sees; the slave is seen. Puella is nominative (subject), servum is accusative (object). The endings decide, regardless of word order.
3

Verbs: the present tense, 1st conjugation, and esse

▾

Latin verbs don't need pronouns: the ending is the pronoun. The 1st conjugation (the -āre family, e.g. amāre, to love) conjugates like this in the present:

PersonSingularPlural
1st (I / we)amō — I loveamāmus — we love
2nd (you)amās — you loveamātis — you (all) love
3rd (he, she, it / they)amat — he/she lovesamant — they love

Learn the six endings as a chant: -ō, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt. They attach to nearly every Latin verb you'll meet: vocō (I call), laudat (he praises), labōrant (they work), pugnāmus (we fight).

The one verb you must know cold is esse, to be — it's irregular, and it's everywhere:

SingularPlural
1stsum — I amsumus — we are
2ndes — you areestis — you (all) are
3rdest — he/she/it issunt — they are

New verbs to memorize: vocō (call), laudō (praise), pugnō (fight), labōrō (work), ambulō (walk), portō (carry).

Try
Conjugate laudō (I praise) fully, aloud, then translate: Servī dominum laudant. Poēta ambulat et canit. (canit = sings — 3rd conjugation, but the -t ending still tells you "he/she sings")
Check
Why does Amāmus Rōmam need no word for "we"?
The -mus ending already means "we" — the subject is built into the verb. ("We love Rome.") Latin adds explicit pronouns like nōs only for emphasis or contrast.
4

Building sentences: word order, adjectives, prepositions

▾

Latin's default word order is subject – object – verb: Puella servum vocat ("The girl the slave calls"). The verb goes last; the first and last positions are the emphatic ones, so Romans moved words there for stress. Case endings keep the meaning exact no matter the order.

Adjectives agree with the noun they describe in gender, number, and case — three things at once. Bonus (good) is a 1st/2nd-declension adjective:

MasculineFeminineNeuter
Nom. sg.bonusbonabonum
Acc. sg.bonumbonambonum

So: puella bona (a good girl), servus bonus (a good slave), bellum bonum (a good war — well, a successful one). And in a sentence: Puella bona servum malum vocat — "The good girl calls the bad slave." The adjective's ending, not its position, tells you which noun it belongs to.

Prepositions govern a case — they demand accusative or ablative after them:

inablativein, onin forō — in the forum
inaccusativeintoin forum — into the forum
adaccusativeto, towardad urbem — to the city
ab / āablativefrom, away fromab urbe — away from the city
cumablativewithcum amīcīs — with friends
sineablativewithoutsine metū — without fear
peraccusativethroughper urbem — through the city
post / anteaccusativeafter / beforepost bellum — after the war

New adjectives: malus (bad), magnus (great, big), parvus (small), novus (new), multus (much, many).

Try
Translate: Magna puella parvum servum in forō videt. Amīcī cum poētā ad urbem ambulant. Then rearrange the first sentence's words and confirm the meaning doesn't change.
Check
In forō vs. in forum — what's the difference?
Case. In with the ablative means "in, on" (location — standing in the forum); in with the accusative means "into" (motion — walking into the forum). The preposition is the same; the case carries the distinction.

Latin alive in English. You speak fragments of Latin daily:

  • Law: habeas corpus ("you shall have the body" — the right against unlawful detention), subpoena (sub + poena, "under penalty"), pro bono ("for the good" — free legal work), versus ("turned against").
  • Science: Homo sapiens ("wise man"), terra firma ("solid earth"), and every species name ever published.
  • Mottos: Harvard's Veritas ("truth"), tempus fugit ("time flies"), caveat emptor ("let the buyer beware"), alma mater ("nourishing mother" — your school).
Try
Drill the 60 with the flashcards on the Review page, nouns and verbs first. Then decode three real phrases from the lists above without help: pro bono publico, terra incognita, magna cum laude.
Check
What does subpoena literally break into, and what does that tell you about what it is?
Sub (under) + poena (penalty) — "under penalty." It's a court order you obey or face punishment. (Poena is also the root of English "pain" and "penalty" — Grammar's roots lesson in action.)
★

Capstone: Caesar's Gaul and a real Roman inscription

▾

Two translations. Use everything from lessons 1–5; every word you haven't met is glossed.

1. Caesar, simplified. Julius Caesar opened his account of the Gallic Wars with the most famous first sentence in Latin prose: Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partēs trēs — "Gaul as a whole is divided into three parts." Here's a simplified passage in his style:

Caesar in Galliam vēnit. Gallia est terra magna. In Galliā multī populī habitant. Gallī fortiter pugnant. Caesar cum legiōnibus Rōmānīs in Galliā pugnat. Tandem Rōmānī Gallōs vincunt.

Gloss: Gallia = Gaul (modern France); vēnit = came; terra = land; habitant = they live, dwell; fortiter = bravely; legiō = legion (a Roman army unit); tandem = finally, at last; vincunt = they conquer, defeat; Gallōs = the Gauls (accusative plural).

2. A real inscription. This is carved on the Pantheon in Rome, built under Augustus and rebuilt under Hadrian — you can go read it today:

M·AGRIPPA·L·F·COS·III·FECIT

Gloss: M. = Marcus (a first name); AGRIPPA = Agrippa (his family name); L·F = Lucī fīlius = "son of Lucius" (Romans recorded their father's name); COS = consul (the highest Roman office); III = three times; FECIT = he built, he made. The raised dots separate words — Roman inscriptions had no spaces as we know them.

Try
Copy the Pantheon inscription by hand, expanding every abbreviation into full words: Marcus Agrippa Luci filius consul tertium fecit. Then find one Latin inscription or motto in your own town (a courthouse, a church, a university seal) and decode it.
Check
Translate the full Caesar passage into English.
Caesar came into Gaul. Gaul is a large land. Many peoples live in Gaul. The Gauls fight bravely. Caesar fights in Gaul with the Roman legions. Finally the Romans defeat the Gauls. (And the inscription: "Marcus Agrippa, son of Lucius, consul three times, built this.")
