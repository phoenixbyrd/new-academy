1

The Greek ideal

▾

Objectives

  • Explain why the Greeks treated physical training as part of education (paideia), not as recreation.
  • Describe kalokagathia — the "beautiful-and-good" ideal — and how it shaped Greek attitudes toward the body.
  • Connect regular movement to the cognitive capacities (attention, memory, judgment) that every other course in this academy requires.

Paideia had two wings

Greek paideia — the formation of a person — had two wings: music (everything we now call the humanities) for the soul, and gymnastics for the body. Boys trained daily in the palaestra; athletes were civic heroes; even philosophers walked while teaching (the Peripatetics — Aristotle's school — literally means "the walkers"). Juvenal's famous line, mens sana in corpore sano — "a sound mind in a sound body" — was originally a prayer: this is what you should ask the gods for.

The word gymnastics comes from gymnos, "naked" — athletes trained unclothed, and the Greeks saw nothing odd in it: the body was not a private embarrassment but a public work in progress. Every Greek city of any size had a gymnasium, and it was as much a school, club, and debating hall as a training ground. Old men went to talk philosophy in the shade while the young wrestled in the dust. Plato, in the Laws, goes so far as to define the uneducated man as one untrained in both music and gymnastics — leave out either wing and the person never gets off the ground.

Kalokagathia: the beautiful-and-good person

The Greek ideal has a name: kalokagathia, from kalos (beautiful) and agathos (good). A kalos kagathos — a "beautiful-and-good" person — was someone in whom excellence of body and excellence of character were two faces of the same thing. This was not vanity. The Greeks believed that courage, discipline, endurance, and grace under pressure were physical virtues first: you learned to stand your ground in the wrestling ring long before you learned to stand your ground in an argument.

That is why athletes were civic heroes. An Olympic victor might be granted free meals for life, a statue in his hometown, and a hero's welcome at the city gates — honors we now reserve for generals and statesmen. The body, trained and tested, was evidence of the soul's quality. Neglecting it was therefore not a lifestyle choice; it was leaving part of your education unfinished, the way skipping grammar would be.

A prayer, not a slogan

Juvenal's line is usually quoted as self-help advice. Read it in context — Satire X, a long poem about the foolish things people pray for (power, beauty, long life) — and it lands differently. After cataloguing every prayer that backfires, Juvenal concludes that the one thing worth asking the gods for is health of mind and body together. It was never a gym motto. It was a philosophy of what to want.

Plato made the same point as curriculum design. He warned that music alone makes a man soft and dreamy, while gymnastics alone makes him hard and savage — and that only the right blend produces a complete person:

"He who mingles music with gymnastic in the fairest proportions, and best attempers them to the soul, may be rightly called the true musician and harmonist in a far higher sense than the tuner of strings."
— Plato, Republic

Notice the standard: not excellence in one wing or the other, but the blend. A scholar who neglects the body and an athlete who neglects the mind have made the same error in opposite directions.

What modern research adds

The modern science agrees with the gymnasium: regular movement improves cognition, mood, sleep, and stress resilience — the very faculties every other course in this academy depends on. Training isn't vanity or a separate hobby. It's maintenance on the instrument you think with.

The evidence is steady rather than miraculous, and you should hold it that way: regular aerobic exercise is consistently associated with better attention, faster learning, and more stable mood. Blood flow, growth factors, and better sleep each explain part of it. Nobody serious claims exercise makes you a genius; the claim is humbler and more useful — that a body in regular motion gives the mind fewer bad days. The Greeks didn't have the mechanism, but they had the observation, and they built their whole educational system around it.

Try
  1. Today: one 10-minute walk. That's it — the minimum viable training. Notice your thinking afterward; the Greeks weren't theorizing about the mind-body link, they were exploiting it.
  2. Repeat it tomorrow and the next day. Three days in a row plants the seed of a habit; one day is an experiment.
  3. After one of the walks, write a single sentence about how your thinking felt afterward. Keep the note where you'll see it — that sentence is your personal evidence for the rest of this course.

Movement foundations: the five patterns

▾

Objectives

  • Perform the five fundamental movement patterns — squat, hinge, push, pull, carry — with safe unloaded form.
  • Explain why the hinge must be learned before any load is added to it.
  • Identify your weakest pattern, so you know where your training should start.

A safety note before you move

Read this once and remember it for the whole course. Stop if something hurts. Muscle effort and mild fatigue are normal; sharp pain, joint pain, dizziness, or chest pain are not — stop immediately, and see a doctor about anything that doesn't resolve quickly or that worries you. Work around injuries, never through them, and progress more slowly than your enthusiasm suggests. This lesson is general educational information, not medical advice: if you have a heart condition, a joint problem, high blood pressure, or are pregnant or recovering from surgery, check with a clinician before starting any training program. The Greeks admired courage, not recklessness.

The five patterns, with form cues

All human strength reduces to five movement patterns. Master these unloaded and you've built the base for everything else:

  • Squat — knees track over toes, chest up, full depth if your joints allow. The pattern of sitting, lifting, living. Cue: screw your feet into the floor and imagine spreading the ground apart as you stand; keep your weight over mid-foot, not on your toes.
  • Hinge — hips push back, back flat, like closing a car door with your hips. The pattern that protects your lower back for life. Drill it against a wall: stand an arm's length away, reach your hips back until your glutes touch the wall, knees soft. Your shins should stay nearly vertical.
  • Push — push-up position: straight line from head to heels, chest to floor. Upper-body pushing strength. Cue: squeeze your glutes and brace your abs so your hips don't sag — a push-up is a moving plank.
  • Pull — the hardest to train without equipment; a sturdy table edge for rows, or a dead hang from anything solid overhead. Cue: hang with shoulders down away from your ears, not shrugged; for rows, pull your chest to the bar or table edge and pinch your shoulder blades together.
  • Carry — walk while holding weight (grocery bags count). Grip, core, and posture in one ancient exercise. Cue: stand tall, ribs down, shoulders packed — don't let the weight drag you into a hunch.

The Greek wrestlers and pentathletes built formidable bodies with nothing but these patterns, progressively loaded. Equipment is optional; the patterns are not. Notice that every pattern is something you already do: you squat to sit, hinge to pick things up, push doors, pull them open, and carry groceries. Training is just doing these five things deliberately, with better form and gradually more demand.

Pattern first, load second

Strength is a skill before it is a capacity. Your nervous system has to learn the movement — which muscles fire, in what order — and it learns by repetition. Roughly a hundred clean, unloaded repetitions grooves a pattern deeply enough that adding load builds strength instead of building compensation. Loaded sloppily, a squat trains your knees to cave and your back to round; the strength you gain then belongs to the bad pattern, and you'll have to unlearn it later at greater cost. The palaestra understood this instinctively: beginners drilled forms for months before they were allowed to wrestle in earnest.

Galen's whole-body test

Two thousand years before the modern gym, the physician Galen (c. 129–210 AD) — team doctor to gladiators, author of the great ancient treatise On Hygiene — drew a line that still matters. He distinguished true gymnastics, exercise aimed at health and the whole body, from the corrupted version: training that chased performance or appearance at the expense of health.

"We should summon instead those who are proficient in true gymnastics — those who gained an understanding of the overall art concerning the body."
— Galen, Thrasymachus (trans. P. N. Singer)

His context was a quarrel with athletic trainers who, in his view, wrecked their charges' health for the sake of victory. His standard was wholeness: the best exercise works the entire body moderately and vigorously, not one part obsessively. Run your own training through Galen's test. Five patterns, whole body, no neglected corners — that is true gymnastics. A program that builds a huge chest and a fragile back would have failed his inspection, and it should fail yours.

Try
  1. Right now, unloaded: 10 bodyweight squats, 10 hip hinges, 5 incline push-ups (hands on a counter), a 20-second dead hang or 30-second farmer's carry with two bags. Note which pattern feels weakest — that's your starting point.
  2. Practice 10 wall-touch hinges: stand an arm's length from a wall, push your hips straight back until your glutes touch it, return. Slow and smooth — this is the spinal-safety drill worth repeating daily for a week.
  3. Hold a 30-second plank in front of a mirror (or film yourself). Check the straight line from head to heels. If your hips sag, drop to your knees and hold the line there — a short perfect plank beats a long sagging one.
Check
Why learn the hinge before adding any load to it?
Because the hinge is a spinal-safety skill: hips back, back flat, load on the hips and hamstrings. Loading a bad hinge pattern trains injury. Pattern first, load second — always.
Check
Which pattern is hardest to train without equipment, and why?
The pull. Squats, hinges, and push-ups all use your body weight against the floor, which is always available — but pulling requires something to pull against or hang from: a sturdy table, a solid bar, a doorframe-mounted bar. Gravity gives you the push for free; the pull needs an anchor.
Check
What does Galen's "true gymnastics" rule out?
Training that sacrifices health or wholeness for performance or appearance — over-specializing one body part, pushing through damage, or neglecting the rest of the body. True gymnastics works the whole body moderately and vigorously, in service of health.

Summary

Five patterns — squat, hinge, push, pull, carry — learned unloaded before any load is added, checked against Galen's whole-body standard, and practiced with a clear safety rule: stop if something hurts. Master these and you own the base that every later lesson builds on.

Walking and running: the ancient base

▾

Objectives

  • Explain what an aerobic base is and why the Greeks built theirs automatically.
  • Use the talk test to find your own easy (zone 2) pace.
  • Set up a weekly walking habit before adding any intensity.

The Greeks walked everywhere

The Greeks walked everywhere — between cities, to the gymnasium, while philosophizing. Walking was the aerobic base of ancient life, and modern physiology confirms the prescription: zone 2 cardio (a pace where you can talk in full sentences but slightly prefer not to) builds the mitochondrial engine that powers everything else. Most people skip the base and chase intensity; the ancients built the base by living it.

Consider what a day demanded: a craftsman walked to his workshop, an athlete walked to the gymnasium and back, a philosopher walked while lecturing, a messenger might run between cities. Nobody "did cardio." The base accumulated the way interest accrues in a savings account — quietly, constantly. We have to schedule deliberately what they got by default, which is exactly what this lesson is for.

Zone 2: the talking-pace engine

Easy aerobic work — roughly 60–70% of your maximum heart rate — trains your cells' mitochondria to burn fuel efficiently, grows the capillary networks that deliver oxygen, and teaches your body to use fat as fuel. The practical test needs no device: the talk test. At the right pace you can speak in full sentences, but you'd slightly prefer not to; you can breathe through your nose the whole time. If you're gasping, you're too fast. If you could sing, you're strolling — pleasant, but below the training zone.

The public-health target is a useful floor: about 150 minutes a week of moderate activity. For a beginner that translates to roughly 30 brisk minutes, five days a week. Don't optimize this yet — no heart-rate monitor, no app required. Pace by breath and speech, and accumulate time. The engine you build here is what later lets you recover between hard efforts, think clearly under fatigue, and sustain a long day. Everything fast is built on top of everything easy.

Base before intensity

The rule: aerobic base before intensity. Walk briskly 30+ minutes most days before you worry about running fast. When walking feels easy, add short intervals — one minute brisk, two minutes easy, repeated. The Greek pentathletes ran the stadion (about 200 meters) and the diaulos; you can start with the block.

The ancient footraces show the same base-to-peak logic. The stadion — one length of the stadium, about 192 meters at Olympia — was the original and most prestigious event. The diaulos doubled it, down and back. The dolichos was the long race, many laps of the stadium, the ancient equivalent of distance running. Sprinters and distance men alike built their speed on a foundation of daily walking and easy running; the stadium events were the peak of the pyramid, not the whole pyramid.

"Walking is man's best medicine."
— attributed to Hippocrates

The attribution is traditional rather than certain — no one can point to the exact line in the Hippocratic corpus — but the sentiment is authentically Hippocratic: the physicians of the Regimen treatises prescribed walking as central to health, dosed like a drug, matched to diet and constitution. Two and a half millennia later, the prescription hasn't changed. It just comes with better shoes.

Try
  1. A 30-minute walk at a pace where you can breathe through your nose the whole time. If you can't, slow down — that's the correct intensity, and holding it is the discipline.
  2. Walk the same route three times this week and note your time and how it felt. You're looking for the route to start feeling easier — that's the base building, and it's measurable within two weeks.
  3. Only once easy walking feels genuinely comfortable: finish one walk with 5 rounds of 1 minute brisk / 2 minutes easy. If the brisk minutes leave you gasping, return to pure walking for another two weeks.
Check

Summary

Walk briskly at a talking pace, about 30 minutes most days, before you touch intensity — the stadion, diaulos, and dolichos all sat atop the same kind of base. Build the engine first; speed is a later lesson.

Strength without a gym

▾

Objectives

  • Tell the story of Milo of Croton and state the principle it illustrates: progressive overload.
  • Locate your current rung on each of the four strength ladders.
  • Apply the 3×8 → 3×12 protocol to move up rungs safely.

Milo and the growing calf

The patron saint of progressive overload is Milo of Croton, the most famous wrestler of antiquity — a six-time Olympic champion in the sixth century BC, a man of such legendary strength that stories about him were still being told five hundred years later. The geographer Pausanias records that Milo once carried a full-grown bull on his shoulders around the stadium at Olympia. And the story goes that he had begun by lifting the animal as a newborn calf, hoisting it a little higher and carrying it a little farther each day as it grew — until the calf was a bull and Milo was a legend.

The calf grew a little every day, and Milo grew a little every day to match it — strength built not in a single heroic effort but in hundreds of unremarkable ones.
— the traditional account of Milo of Croton's training

Strip away the legend and the principle is exactly what modern training science calls progressive overload: gradually demanding slightly more than last time. The body adapts to the demand placed on it — no more, no less. Demand a little more than you're used to, recover, and the new level becomes the baseline. Repeat for months and the accumulation looks like a miracle; it is actually arithmetic.

Strength grows by progressive overload: gradually demanding slightly more than last time. No gym required — bodyweight offers built-in progressions, rungs on a ladder:

Find the hardest rung where you can do clean reps — that is your working rung. Everything below it is warm-up; everything above it is the future. Note that the hinge ladder ends with loaded bags rather than a flashier bodyweight trick: the hinge is a loaded pattern by nature, and a bag of books is a perfectly respectable barbell.

The 3×8 → 3×12 protocol

The protocol the Greeks would recognize: pick the hardest rung you can do for 3 sets of 8 clean reps, train 2–3 times a week, and move up a rung when 3×12 feels solid. Clean reps only — the palaestra punished sloppy form, and so should you.

In practice, a session looks like this: warm up with five minutes of easy movement, then work through your four ladders — push, squat, hinge, pull — doing 3 sets of each at your working rung, resting 60–90 seconds between sets. Two or three such sessions a week, never on consecutive days at first. When you can complete 3 sets of 12 clean reps at your rung, you have owned it; test the next rung, and if you can manage 3×8 there, it becomes the new working rung. If you stall for three weeks, drop one rung and rebuild — the ladder is still there.

Why full-body, two to three times a week? Because as a beginner your fastest gains come from practice frequency: each session is another hundred clean repetitions grooving the pattern into your nervous system. Advanced lifters split their training because they need enormous volume per muscle; you need skill, and skill wants frequency. Milo didn't lift the calf once a week.

Try
  1. Find your current rung on push-ups and squats: test the hardest variation you can do for 8 clean reps. That's your starting rung — write it down. Retest in two weeks.
  2. Run one full session at your rungs: 3 sets each of push, squat, hinge, and pull, resting 60–90 seconds between sets. Note the reps you actually completed — that log is your training program.
  3. Do the same session twice more this week on non-consecutive days. Three sessions in, compare your numbers to day one; early progress is mostly the nervous system learning, and it's real progress.
Check
Why move up a rung only when 3×12 feels solid, rather than as soon as you can do one ugly rep of the next rung?
Because strength is a skill practiced through perfect repetitions — ugly reps train bad patterns and invite injury. Owning a rung (3×12 clean) means the pattern is grooved; the next rung then builds on solid ground instead of compensation.
Check
What principle does the story of Milo and the calf illustrate?
Progressive overload: the body adapts to gradually increasing demand. Milo added a little each day as the calf grew, so each day's effort was only slightly beyond the last — and the accumulation over months built legendary strength.
Check
Why should beginners train full-body 2–3 times a week instead of splitting muscles across days?
Because early gains come mostly from motor learning — grooving clean movement patterns — and skill improves with frequency. Each full-body session is another round of quality practice on every pattern; splits are for advanced lifters who need huge volume per muscle.

Summary

Strength is progressive overload applied to clean patterns: find your rung on each ladder, work 3×8, own it at 3×12, climb. Milo's calf is the whole program — a little more than last time, repeated until it looks like legend.

Recovery: sleep and simple food

▾

Objectives

  • Explain why adaptation happens during recovery, not during the workout.
  • Set up a consistent sleep schedule as the first pillar of recovery.
  • Apply the ancient athlete's simple-food template to your own meals.

The workout is the question; recovery is the answer

The ancient athletes understood what modern science keeps rediscovering: you don't get stronger from training — you get stronger from recovering from training. The workout is the stimulus; sleep and food are the adaptation. Two pillars, both unglamorous:

Think of it as a transaction. Training spends: it fatigues muscles, depletes fuel, and disrupts the body's equilibrium. Recovery repays with interest: given sleep and food, the body doesn't just return to baseline — it overshoots slightly, building a little more capacity than it had before. Physiologists call this supercompensation, and it is the entire mechanism of getting fitter. No recovery, no overshoot; chronic under-recovery means you keep spending from an account you never refill, and performance quietly declines. Treat recovery as part of training, scheduled with the same seriousness — because physiologically, it is.

Sleep: the first pillar

  • Sleep: 7–9 hours, consistent times. Sleep is when growth hormone peaks, memories consolidate (your Logic and Grammar lessons included), and the nervous system resets. One bad night measurably degrades strength, reaction time, and judgment.

Consistency matters as much as duration: the same bedtime and wake time every day — weekends included — trains your circadian rhythm the way sets and reps train a muscle. Practical rules that actually work: screens off 30 minutes before bed (the light and the scrolling both keep the brain switched on), a cool dark room, no caffeine after midday, and no intense training in the last two hours before sleep. If your mind races, keep a notepad by the bed and empty the worries onto paper; the ancient equivalent was probably simpler, but the principle — a settled mind sleeps — is the same.

Note the ordering: sleep first, food second. You can survive imperfect eating far longer than imperfect sleeping. One short night measurably degrades reaction time, mood, and judgment the next day — which is why the Greeks, who had no word for "overtraining," would still have recognized the exhausted athlete who trains hard and sleeps little as a man pouring water into a cracked jar.

Food: the second pillar

  • Food: the Greek athlete's diet was famously simple — figs, cheese, bread, meat. The modern translation: enough protein (roughly a palm-sized portion per meal), mostly whole foods, and enough total fuel to recover. No mysticism required.

The Hippocratic physicians were already prescribing the balance between food and exercise twenty-four centuries ago:

"Eating alone will not keep a man well; he must also take exercise. For food and exercise, while possessing opposite properties, yet contribute mutually to maintain health."
— Hippocrates, On Regimen

Their word for the whole project was diaita — regimen, a way of life — and their prescription was proportion: match your food to your activity, your age, and your constitution. The famous athlete's diet of the classical period was famously plain: figs for energy, cheese and meat for protein, bread for bulk, wine watered. No superfoods, no powders, no mysticism — just enough of the right things, consistently.

The modern translation is equally plain. Protein: roughly a palm-sized portion at each meal gives your body the material to repair what training broke down — meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils all count. Whole foods mostly: if it grew, walked, or swam, it's probably fine; the more processed the food, the easier it is to overeat and the less it satisfies. Enough total fuel: training on too little food doesn't make you tough, it makes you tired — recovery requires energy, and chronic under-eating stalls progress. Water: drink to thirst plus a glass; your urine should be pale. That's the whole diet chapter. Anyone selling you more is selling you something.

Try
  1. Set a fixed bedtime and keep it 7 nights in a row — same time, screens off 30 minutes before. Note your energy on day 7 versus day 1. This is the cheapest performance enhancer in existence.
  2. Add a palm-sized portion of protein to breakfast every day this week. Notice whether your mid-morning energy steadies — most people are under-fueled at the day's start.
  3. Pick one processed snack you eat regularly and replace it with a whole-food version for the week — fruit instead of candy, nuts instead of chips. One swap, not a revolution.
Check
Why is sleep called "part of training" rather than just rest?
Because the adaptations training aims at — muscle repair, neural grooving of movement patterns, memory consolidation — physically occur during sleep. Skipping sleep doesn't just rest less; it cancels part of the training's effect.
Check
What does the Hippocratic quote about food and exercise actually prescribe?
Proportion and partnership: neither food alone nor exercise alone keeps a person well — they "contribute mutually to maintain health." The prescription is to match food to activity as a way of life (diaita), not to chase either one in isolation.
Check
Why is consistency of bedtime as important as total hours?
Because the circadian rhythm — the body's internal clock governing hormones, temperature, and alertness — entrains to regular timing. A regular schedule lets the body anticipate sleep and wake, deepening sleep quality; irregular hours keep the clock permanently jet-lagged even when total hours look fine.

Summary

Training spends; recovery repays with interest. Sleep 7–9 hours on a fixed schedule, eat simply with protein at every meal, and treat both as part of the program — because the adaptation you train for happens there, not in the workout.

Capstone: your pentathlon

▾

Objectives

  • Complete five baseline fitness tests covering endurance, strength, and mobility.
  • Record your scores with today's date in one permanent place.
  • Set a monthly retest date and identify the event that most needs work.

The ancient pentathlon

The ancient pentathlon tested the complete athlete: running, jumping, discus, javelin, wrestling. In 708 BC it entered the Olympic program as the contest for the most versatile athlete in Greece — not the fastest, not the strongest, but the best across five different demands. The five events were:

The stadion race (one length of the stadium, about 192 meters); wrestling; the long jump, performed holding stone or lead jumping-weights (halteres); the javelin throw, launched with a leather strap (the ankyle) wound around the shaft; and the discus throw.
— the ancient Olympic pentathlon program, from 708 BC

The design is the lesson. A specialist could dominate one event and lose the pentathlon; the winner was the athlete with no weak event. Aristotle reportedly called pentathletes the most beautiful of athletes, precisely because their training produced proportion rather than deformity — Galen's "true gymnastics" as a competitive format. Your modern pentathlon below copies the philosophy, not the implements: five tests, five different qualities, no hiding.

Your five events

Safety first. Warm up for 5–10 minutes before testing — easy walking, arm circles, leg swings, a few slow squats. Stop any event if something hurts: sharp pain, dizziness, or chest pain means the test is over for the day, and anything that doesn't resolve quickly deserves a doctor's attention. These are baseline measurements, not competitions; there is no score worth an injury. This capstone is general fitness information, not medical advice.

The ancient pentathlon tested the complete athlete: running, jumping, discus, javelin, wrestling. Yours tests the complete you — five baseline events, no equipment beyond a timer:

  1. Brisk 1-mile walk or run — time it. Your aerobic base, in one number. Walk it briskly if you're not a runner; either is a valid baseline. Use a measured route (four laps of a standard track is a mile) or a mapping app.
  2. Max good-form push-ups — unbroken, chest to a fist's height. Form breaks = set over. Use your working rung from Lesson 4 (floor push-ups if you have them, incline if not) and note which variation you used so the retest matches.
  3. Bodyweight squats in 2 minutes — full depth, steady pace. Count clean reps. Stop the count when your form degrades; the number you write down is clean reps only.
  4. Dead hang or farmer's carry — hang from anything solid for max time, or carry two heavy bags as far as you can. Pick one and stick with it for future retests. Time the hang; measure the carry in steps or distance.
  5. Sit-and-reach — seated, legs straight, reach past your toes; measure the distance. Your mobility baseline. Warm up first — cold hamstrings lie about your flexibility.

Spread the five events across two days if doing them all at once leaves you too fatigued to give honest efforts — day one: mile, push-ups, squats; day two: hang/carry, sit-and-reach. The scores are only comparable if the effort is honest, so err toward fresh.

Scoring, retesting, and what to do with the numbers

Record all five scores with today's date. Retest monthly. The Greeks competed against others; you compete against last month's you — which is the only opponent that matters.

Why retest monthly rather than weekly?
Because real physiological adaptation takes weeks; weekly scores mostly reflect noise — sleep, stress, weather, motivation. Monthly testing, under the same conditions, shows the actual trend instead of the daily static.
Check
Why did the pentathlon — ancient and modern — test five different qualities instead of one?
Because the goal is the complete athlete, not the specialist. Five different demands expose weak events that single-event training hides; the winner is the person with no weak event, which is also Galen's whole-body standard for healthy training.
Check
What should you do if an event causes sharp pain during testing?
Stop that event immediately — the test is over for the day. Sharp pain, dizziness, or chest pain are stop signals, not obstacles; see a doctor about anything that doesn't resolve quickly. No baseline score is worth an injury.

Summary

Five events, five qualities, one table of scores with today's date, retested monthly. The ancient pentathlon crowned the most versatile athlete; yours crowns the most honest record of your own progress — compete against last month's you.
