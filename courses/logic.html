Trivium II

Logic

The art of thinking well. Arguments have structure; most public reasoning collapses the moment you check the structure.

1

Arguments: premises, conclusions, and the two questions

▾

An argument is a set of statements where some (premises) are offered as support for another (the conclusion). Two questions, in order:

  1. Is it valid? — If the premises were true, would the conclusion have to be true? Validity is about structure, not reality.
  2. Is it sound? — Is it valid and are the premises actually true?

A valid argument can have a false conclusion (bad premises). An invalid argument can have a true conclusion (lucky guess). Only soundness gives you knowledge.

Try
Find an argument in an opinion article. Write out its premises numbered, then its conclusion. Ask the two questions separately — most articles fail at question 2, not question 1.
Check
"All cats are mammals. Whiskers is a cat. Therefore Whiskers is a mammal." Valid? Sound?
Valid — the structure guarantees the conclusion if the premises hold. Sound — the premises are in fact true. (Soundness is always hostage to the real world.)
2

The syllogism and its traps

▾

Aristotle's syllogism is the atom of deductive reasoning:

All humans are mortal.
Socrates is human.
Therefore Socrates is mortal.

Two classic traps to memorize:

  • Affirming the consequent: "If it rains, the ground is wet. The ground is wet. Therefore it rained." Invalid — sprinklers exist.
  • Denying the antecedent: "If it rains, the ground is wet. It didn't rain. Therefore the ground isn't wet." Invalid — same reason.
  • Valid forms: modus ponens (if P then Q; P; therefore Q) and modus tollens (if P then Q; not Q; therefore not P).
Try
Formalize this: "If the policy worked, prices would fall. Prices didn't fall. So the policy didn't work." Which valid form is it?
Check
"If he's guilty he'll flee. He fled. So he's guilty." What's wrong?
Affirming the consequent — an invalid form. Innocent people flee too (fear, panic, bad advice). The conclusion might be true, but the argument doesn't establish it.
3

Fallacies I: attacks on the arguer

▾

Informal fallacies are bad moves that look persuasive. First family: instead of engaging the argument, attack something near it.

  • Ad hominem: "You can't trust his tax plan — he's a convicted fraudster." Maybe true of him; irrelevant to whether the plan works.
  • Strawman: restating the opponent's position in a weaker form, then defeating the restatement.
  • Appeal to authority (illegitimate): citing an expert outside their field, or a vague "studies show."
  • Tu quoque ("you too"): "You criticize pollution but you drive a car." Hypocrisy doesn't refute a claim.
  • Genetic fallacy: judging a claim by its origin — "that idea came from them, so it's wrong."
Try
Watch ten minutes of any televised debate or argument show. Tally each fallacy from this lesson. You'll rarely get past three minutes without one.
Check
Is "experts agree" ever a legitimate appeal to authority?
Yes — when the experts are in the relevant field, there's genuine consensus, and the claim is within their competence. The fallacy is citing authority as a substitute for evidence, or citing the wrong authority.
4

Fallacies II: tricks with the evidence

▾

Second family: the argument looks evidence-based, but the evidence doesn't do the claimed work.

  • Cherry-picking: citing only the data that fits. The antidote: ask what the full dataset shows.
  • False dilemma: "Either we ban it or society collapses." Two options presented where more exist.
  • Slippery slope: "If we allow A, then B, then C, then catastrophe" — with no evidence for the links.
  • Post hoc ergo propter hoc: "After this, therefore because of this." Sequence isn't causation.
  • Hasty generalization: three anecdotes treated as a trend.
  • Begging the question: the conclusion smuggled into a premise — "We must censor misinformation because false claims are dangerous and must be censored."
Try
Take a viral claim from social media and run it through this list. Write down which fallacy it commits — or, if it's clean, what evidence would be needed to support it.
Check
A headline: "Crime up 40% since the new mayor took office." What logical checks apply before believing the implied claim?
Post hoc (timing ≠ causation), cherry-picking (why this window? what about longer trends?), base rates (40% of what?), and confounding causes (was crime already rising?). The number may be accurate and the implication still false.
5

Beyond deduction: induction and abduction

▾

Most real reasoning isn't deductive. Two other modes run daily life:

  • Induction: from observed cases to general rules. "Every swan I've seen is white → all swans are white." Powerful, never certain — one black swan breaks it. Strength depends on sample size, variety, and method.
  • Abduction: inference to the best explanation. "The lawn is wet, the sky is clear, the sprinkler is running → the sprinkler did it." This is how diagnosis, detective work, and science mostly operate.

The Stoic connection: Epictetus's discipline of assent is abduction applied to your own mind — "what's the best explanation of this impression, and what are the rival explanations?"

Try
Take something you believe about a person in your life ("they're angry at me"). List three rival explanations for the same evidence. Notice how the first explanation felt certain before the rivals existed.
Check
Why is "inference to the best explanation" not the same as guessing?
Because it's constrained: the explanation must account for all the evidence, survive rival explanations, and remain revisable when new evidence arrives. Guessing skips all three constraints.
★

Capstone: the fact-checker's teardown

▾

Pick one opinion piece or viral video making a contested claim. Produce a one-page teardown:

  1. State the conclusion in one sentence (in your own words — no strawmanning).
  2. List the premises. Mark each: verified / unverified / false.
  3. Name the argument's form (deductive? inductive? abductive?) and judge validity.
  4. List every fallacy committed, with quotes.
  5. Verdict: what, if anything, does the evidence actually support?

The standard: accurate means the claim is true as stated — not merely that someone said it. "X said Y" verifies only that the claim was made.
