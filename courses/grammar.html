Trivium I

Grammar

The art of reading well. Before you can reason about a text or argue from it, you must be able to take it apart and see how it's built.

1

Why grammar comes first

▾

Objectives

  • Explain in one sentence why the trivium put grammar before logic.
  • Describe Mortimer Adler's four levels of reading and name the level grammar trains.
  • Parse a sentence: label each word's job before deciding what the sentence means.

The trivium's order

The Romans put grammatica first because every other art depends on it: you cannot evaluate an argument you cannot parse, and you cannot learn from a book you read sloppily. The modern reading researcher Mortimer Adler described four levels of reading — elementary, inspectional, analytical, and syntopical. Most adults stall at inspectional: they skim for the gist. Grammar trains the analytical level: slowing down enough to see exactly what a sentence claims.

The medieval schoolmen kept the same order for the same reason. Their trivium was grammar (the art of reading well), logic or dialectic (the art of thinking well), and rhetoric (the art of speaking well). Notice that the sequence is also a sequence of input, processing, and output: first you take language in accurately, then you judge what you have taken in, then you produce your own. Skip the first step and you corrupt the other two — eloquent nonsense is still nonsense, and a valid argument built on a misread premise is just a polished mistake.

This is not abstract. Most bad reasoning in ordinary life is not bad logic; it is bad reading. Someone reads "studies link coffee to longevity" as "coffee makes you live longer," then argues confidently from the stronger claim. The logic of their argument may be flawless. The reading was not. Grammar is input hygiene: the discipline of establishing exactly what was said before you decide what you think of it.

The four levels of reading

Adler's levels, from How to Read a Book (1940), are worth naming because they give you a map of where you are. Elementary reading is decoding: sounding out words, the thing you learned in first grade. Inspectional reading is systematic skimming: the table of contents, the headings, the first and last paragraphs, used to decide whether a text deserves more of your time. Analytical reading is thorough, chewing reading: you ask what the author claims, what evidence is offered, and whether the argument holds. Syntopical reading is reading several books on one question and constructing an analysis none of them contains alone.

Inspectional reading is a fine servant and a bad master. It is how you triage a crowded inbox or a long feed. But most adults apply it everywhere — to contracts, to news, to medical forms — and then discover they agreed to something they never actually read. Grammar is the ladder from the second level to the third: the set of mechanical moves that turns skimming into seeing.

Parsing: the ancient drill

The ancients learned grammar by parsing — taking sentences apart word by word, naming each word's job. It feels mechanical. That is the point. Mechanics first, interpretation second.

Try it on this: "The exhausted nurse finally finished her shift." Subject: nurse (who did it). Verb: finished (the engine). Object: shift (what got finished). Everything else — the, exhausted, finally, her — is modification. Once you can do this without thinking, long sentences stop being walls of text and start being machines you can disassemble on sight. Roman schoolboys did this to Virgil, line by line, for years. You can do it to the news.

"This profession, then, briefly formulated, comprises two parts: the art of speaking correctly and the explanation of the poets." — Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria 1.4, written around AD 95 as a complete handbook for training an orator from childhood. For Quintilian, grammar is not a side subject; it is the first formal stage of education, the ground every later art stands on. Note his two halves: correctness in your own speech, and understanding in your reading of others. Grammar covers both directions.

Try
  1. Take any paragraph from today's news. Read it once normally. Then read it again and underline the single word in each sentence that carries the claim — usually the main verb. Notice how much of the paragraph is decoration around those verbs.
  2. Take one sentence from a work email and parse it on paper: label the subject, the main verb, the object, and the modifiers. If you cannot find the main verb in ten seconds, the sentence is badly written — rewrite it.
  3. Find a sentence you argued about this week. Rewrite it so its claim is unmistakable, then check: did you change the meaning, or only the fog? Honest rewriting is harder than it looks.
Check
Why did the trivium put grammar before logic?
Because logic evaluates arguments, and you cannot evaluate what you cannot accurately parse. Misreading is the most common source of bad reasoning.
Check
Name Adler's four levels of reading. Which one does grammar train?
Elementary (decoding), inspectional (skimming for structure), analytical (thorough reading that tests claims), and syntopical (reading across books). Grammar trains the analytical level.
Check
What is the difference between parsing and interpreting?
Parsing names each word's job — subject, verb, object, modifiers. Interpreting decides what the sentence means as a whole. Parsing comes first, because interpretation built on mis-parsed structure is guesswork.

Summary

Grammar comes first because every other intellectual art depends on accurate reading. Most adults stall at inspectional skimming; grammar is the mechanical drill — parsing sentences word by word — that builds the analytical level, where you see exactly what a sentence claims before you judge it.

2

Words: the Latin and Greek inside English

▾

Objectives

  • Break an unfamiliar English word into root plus affixes and make a defensible guess at its meaning.
  • Use twenty core Latin and Greek roots to unlock whole families of English words.
  • Explain why English abstract vocabulary is overwhelmingly Latin and Greek in origin.

Why English is two languages in one

Over half of English vocabulary comes from Latin (directly or through French) and Greek — and nearly all of its abstract vocabulary does. Learn twenty roots and thousands of words unlock themselves.

The reason is conquest layered on scholarship. The everyday words — house, water, eat, hand — are Anglo-Saxon, the language of the people who worked the land. In 1066 the Normans conquered England, and French, a daughter of Latin, became the language of law, government, and high culture for three centuries. So English got pairs: the Saxon cow in the field, the French beef on the lord's table; ask versus interrogate, begin versus commence, kingly versus royal. Later, Renaissance scholars building new sciences reached for Greek and Latin to name new ideas — telephone, biology, democracy — because the classical languages were the shared toolkit of educated Europe.

The practical result: if a word feels abstract, technical, or formal, it is probably Latin or Greek underneath. And roots are reusable. English builds words the way bricks build houses: a small set of pieces, combined endlessly. Learn the pieces and you stop memorizing words one by one.

A note on the table: a few roots pull double duty. Log means both word and reason because the Greeks used logos for both — speech is reason made audible. Democracy appears twice on purpose: dem (people) + crat (rule), rule by the people. Once you see the join, you cannot unsee it.

How to dissect a word

Split any unfamiliar word into three slots: prefix (before), root (the core), suffix (after). Then reassemble the meaning. Worked examples:

  • Circumspect = circum- (around) + spec (look) = looking around — careful, watchful.
  • Predict = pre- (before) + dict (say) = say beforehand.
  • Anthropology = anthropos (human) + log (study) = the study of humans. Anthropos is not in the table, but now you own it anyway — and misanthrope (mis-, hate + anthropos) unlocks for free.
  • Transport = trans- (across) + port (carry) = carry across.

Prefixes worth memorizing alongside the roots: pre- (before), re- (again, back), sub- (under), super- (above), circum- (around), trans- (across), bene- (good), mal- (bad), eu- (good), dys- (bad), a-/an- (not), anti- (against), syn-/sym- (together). Watch for traps: inflammable means flammable (the in- is intensive, not negative), and contradict is contra- (against) + dict (say) — to speak against.

Words as signs

"A sign is a thing which, over and above the impression it makes on the senses, causes something else to come into the mind." — Augustine, De doctrina christiana 2.1, written around AD 397. Augustine treats words as signs: marks that point past themselves to things. A root is a sign with a long history — when you learn that dict means "say," every word built on it starts pointing at the same action, and your vocabulary becomes a system instead of a pile. That is what the ancients were doing when they taught etymology: not trivia, but learning to see through the word to the thing.

Try
  1. Decode these without a dictionary: circumspect, benevolent, anthropology, misanthrope, predict, transport. Break each into root + prefix, then check yourself.
  2. Coin a word from roots — chronophobia, malediction, anything — write a one-line definition, then test it on someone: can they guess the meaning cold? If yes, the system works.
  3. Open any article and find five abstract words. Trace each to a root in the table or a prefix from this lesson. Count how many you could now explain to a ten-year-old.
Check
What does eudaimonia break into, roughly?
Eu- (good) + daimon (spirit/fortune) — literally "having a good guiding spirit," usually translated as flourishing or living well. It's the central goal-word of Greek ethics.
Check
Decode benevolent and misanthrope.
Bene- (good) + vol (wish) = wishing well. Mis- (hate) + anthropos (human) = hater of humankind.
Check
Why does learning roots beat memorizing definitions one word at a time?
Roots transfer. One root unlocks a whole family of words you have never seen — so twenty roots cover thousands of words, including words that have not been coined yet.

Summary

English abstract vocabulary is mostly Latin and Greek because of the Norman conquest and Renaissance scholarship. Twenty roots, combined with a dozen prefixes, let you dissect unfamiliar words instead of memorizing them — turning vocabulary from a pile of facts into a system of signs.

3

Sentences: structure is meaning

▾

Objectives

  • Reduce any sentence to its skeleton: subject, main verb, object.
  • Label modifiers and explain what each one adds — or hides.
  • Spot the three common traps: passive voice, nominalization, buried subjects.

The sentence as machine

Every sentence is a small machine: a subject doing something (verb), usually to something (object), with modifiers bolted on. Most confusion in reading comes from losing track of the machine inside long sentences. The ancient exercise was parsing: label every word's function.

A practical modern version — the "skeleton" method:

  1. Find the main verb (the sentence's engine).
  2. Ask "who/what?" before it — that's the subject.
  3. Ask "whom/what?" after it — that's the object.
  4. Everything else is modification: cross it out and check the skeleton still stands.

Example: "The committee, after three hours of bitter and largely pointless debate, approved the budget." Skeleton: committee approved budget. Nine words of fog around three words of meaning.

Worked parse: a longer sentence

Take this: "The senator, who had opposed the bill for months, reluctantly voted in favor of it at dawn."

Step one, the engine: voted. Step two, who voted: senator. Step three, voted what: nothing direct — in favor of it is a prepositional phrase doing the object's work. Skeleton: senator voted (in favor of it). Now the modifiers: the (which senator — definite, known), who had opposed the bill for months (a whole clause bolted on, explaining the reluctance), reluctantly (how), at dawn (when). Cross them all out and the claim still stands; put them back and you see the story. The skeleton tells you what happened. The modifiers tell you why it matters — and sometimes they are doing quiet persuasive work, like reluctantly, which asks you to admire the senator's sacrifice.

Three traps that hide the machine

TrapExampleWhat to do
Passive voice hides the actor"Mistakes were made during the audit."Ask: by whom? Rewrite it active and see what changes.
Nominalization buries the verb"The implementation of the policy failed."Unpack the noun into a verb: who implemented what, and how did it fail?
Buried subject"After months of debate, during which every option was exhausted, the committee voted."Find the verb first, then ask who — ignore the runway.

These are not grammar crimes; they are tools, and tools get used. Politicians love the passive because it deletes responsibility. Bureaucracies love nominalizations because verbs commit you and nouns do not. Your job as a reader is not to ban them but to notice them — to restore the actor and the action the sentence was engineered to blur.

The oldest definition of grammar

"Grammar is an acquaintance with the usages of language as generally current among poets and prose writers." — Dionysius Thrax, Art of Grammar, 2nd century BC, the earliest surviving Greek grammar. Note what he does not say: grammar is not a list of rules handed down from above. It is disciplined observation of how language is actually used — by the best writers, in real sentences. Parsing is that observation made into a habit.

Try
  1. Take a long sentence from a terms-of-service agreement or a political speech. Reduce it to its skeleton. Then ask: does the skeleton say anything the fog was hiding?
  2. Find three passive-voice sentences in the wild and rewrite each in the active voice. Note what the passive was hiding — usually an actor, sometimes an admission.
  3. Parse one sentence from a poem you like. Notice what survives the skeleton and what the modifiers were doing — in poetry, the "decoration" is often the point.
Check
What's the skeleton of: "Critics of the proposal, citing concerns about cost and fairness, have vowed to block it."?
Critics have vowed (to block it). Everything else — "of the proposal," "citing concerns about cost and fairness" — is modification.
Check
Who made the mistakes in "Mistakes were made during the audit"?
You cannot tell from the sentence — the passive voice with no agent deletes the actor. That deletion is the thing to notice; the grammar is hiding the responsibility.
Check
What is a nominalization, and why does it matter for a reader?
A verb turned into a noun — "implementation," "failure to comply." It buries the action and often the actor. Unpack it back into a verb to find out who did what.

Summary

Every sentence is a machine: subject, verb, object, plus modifiers. The skeleton method strips a sentence to its claim in four steps, and knowing the three traps — passive voice, nominalization, buried subjects — lets you see when a sentence is engineered to hide its own meaning.

Latin primer I: how the language works

▾

Objectives

  • Explain what inflection is and why it frees Latin word order.
  • Decline puella through five cases and conjugate amo in the present tense.
  • Translate short Latin sentences using the core vocabulary.

The big idea: endings do the work

Latin is worth meeting even if you never become fluent: it trains precision, unlocks Romance languages, and lets you read two thousand years of inscriptions, mottos, and law in the original. The core idea: Latin marks a word's job with its ending, so word order is flexible and meaning is exact.

  • Nouns: puella (girl) changes ending by case — puella (subject), puellam (object), puellae (of the girl / to the girl).
  • Verbs: amo = I love, amas = you love, amat = he/she loves. The ending carries the pronoun.
  • So: Puella poetam amat = "The girl loves the poet" — and Poetam puella amat means the same thing, because the endings, not the order, assign the roles.

English does the same jobs with word order and little helper words. "The girl loves the poet" means one thing; "the poet loves the girl" means the reverse — position is everything. Latin puts the job into the word itself, which is why a Roman could scramble the order for emphasis, rhythm, or drama without changing who did what to whom.

Cases: the jobs a noun can do

Latin has six cases; five do most of the work. Here is puella (girl, first declension) all the way through:

CaseFormJobEnglish equivalent
Nominativepuellasubjectthe girl (does it)
Genitivepuellaeofof the girl
Dativepuellaeto / forto the girl
Accusativepuellamdirect objectthe girl (receives it)
Ablativepuellaby / with / fromby the girl, with the girl

Notice that puellae does double duty (genitive and dative) — context decides. This is normal in inflected languages: the system is economical, not perfectly one-to-one. The sixth case, the vocative (direct address — "O girl!"), matches the nominative here, so puella covers it too. Learn the pattern once and it repeats across thousands of nouns: first-declension endings are -a, -ae, -ae, -am, -a, and you will meet them everywhere.

Verbs: the ending is the pronoun

The present tense of amo (I love):

SingularPlural
1st personamo (I love)amamus (we love)
2nd personamas (you love)amatis (you all love)
3rd personamat (he/she/it loves)amant (they love)

The endings — -o, -s, -t, -mus, -tis, -nt — are the real pronouns. Latin drops separate subject words (ego, I; tu, you) except for emphasis: saying ego amo is like saying "I love," with the stress on I. This is why Latin feels compact: a single word, amant, carries what English needs two words for. When you meet new verbs, strip the ending to find the stem (ama-), then read the ending for the person. That one move unlocks every regular verb.

How Romans learned it

"Partes orationis quot sunt? Octo." — "How many parts of speech are there? Eight." — Aelius Donatus, Ars grammatica, 4th century AD. Donatus taught Jerome (the Bible translator), and his little question-and-answer grammar became the school textbook of Europe for a thousand years. Roman boys did not learn Latin from explanations like this lesson; they learned it by chanting paradigms — amo, amas, amat — until the endings lived in their mouths. Memorization first, understanding second: the same mechanics-before-interpretation order as parsing.

First vocabulary (memorize these ten): et (and), non (not), est (is), in (in/on), cum (with), amor (love), veritas (truth), vita (life), mors (death), tempus (time).

Try
  1. Translate: Veritas non verba magistri. (Hint: verba = words, magistri = of the teacher.) Then translate: Tempus et mors non exspectant. (exspectant = they wait.)
  2. Write out the full present conjugation of amo from memory — all six forms — then check against the table. Repeat tomorrow; this is how the endings stick.
  3. Rearrange Puella poetam amat into all six possible word orders. Confirm each means the same thing, and notice which orders sound most dramatic — Romans used position for emphasis, the way English uses italics.
Check
Why does Latin rely less on word order than English?
Because inflected endings mark each word's grammatical role (subject, object, etc.), so position is free for emphasis rather than required for meaning.
Check
Give the accusative singular of puella and say what job it does.
Puellam — it marks the direct object, the receiver of the verb's action.
Check
Why can Latin drop subject pronouns like "I" and "you"?
Because the verb ending already marks person and number — amo is unambiguously "I love" — so a separate pronoun is redundant except for emphasis.

Summary

Latin marks a word's grammatical job with its ending: nouns change by case, verbs by person and number. That inflection is what frees word order and makes meaning exact — and chanting the paradigms until the endings are automatic is exactly how Roman schoolboys learned it.

Latin primer II: reading real Latin

▾

Objectives

  • Translate six famous Latin phrases and explain the history behind each.
  • Distinguish literal translation from idiomatic meaning.
  • Compose original Latin sentences using the vocabulary of lessons 4 and 5.
  • Read Latin aloud with Classical pronunciation.

The phrases hiding in plain sight

More essential vocabulary: sapientia (wisdom), virtus (excellence/virtue), fortuna (fortune/luck), ratio (reason), lex (law), populus (people), senatus (senate), bellum (war), pax (peace), libertas (freedom).

Now read real phrases — these are everywhere once you notice:

  • Veni, vidi, vici. — "I came, I saw, I conquered." (Caesar)
  • Carpe diem. — "Seize the day." (Horace — carpe literally means "pluck," as in fruit)
  • Amor fati. — "Love of one's fate." (The Stoic motto Nietzsche revived)
  • Memento mori. — "Remember you must die." (memento = remember! mori = to die)
  • E pluribus unum. — "Out of many, one."
  • Per aspera ad astra. — "Through hardships to the stars."

From literal to idiomatic

Good translation happens in two passes: literal first, then idiomatic. The literal pass keeps you honest; the idiomatic pass makes it English. Skip the first and you invent meanings; skip the second and you produce word-salad.

Take carpe diem. Literally: "pluck the day" — carpe is what you do to ripe fruit. The metaphor is harvesting a moment at its ripeness, not grabbing it violently. "Seize the day" is the standard idiom, but the literal image deepens it: Horace is telling you the day is perishable, like fruit. That is the kind of thing you only get from the literal pass.

Three more to add to your collection:

  • Alea iacta est. — "The die is cast." What Caesar said (in Greek, as reported) when he crossed the Rubicon in 49 BC, committing himself to civil war. The die, once thrown, cannot be unthrown: the idiom is the point of no return.
  • Si vis pacem, para bellum. — "If you want peace, prepare for war." From Vegetius's De re militari, a Roman army manual. Vis = you want, para = prepare — both from verb endings you can now recognize.
  • Sapere aude. — "Dare to be wise." Horace (Epistles 1.2.40), taken up by Kant as the motto of the Enlightenment: have the courage to use your own reason.

Reading like a Roman schoolboy

Romans learned Latin by reading aloud — silently reading to yourself was rare in antiquity — so pronounce as you go. Classical rules of thumb: v sounds like English w, c is always hard (like k), and ae sounds like "eye." So veni, vidi, vici is "weh-nee, wee-dee, wee-kee." Say it: the triple parallel ending is the whole joke — three verbs, three syllables each, total victory in nine syllables. That is rhetoric inside grammar.

"Tolle lege." — "Take up and read." — Augustine, Confessions 8.12. In a Milan garden in 386, Augustine heard a child's voice chanting these words, opened Paul's letters at random, and read the passage that converted him. However you take the story, it is the tradition's most famous tribute to the act this lesson trains: reading changes the reader.

Try
  1. Using the vocabulary from lessons 4–5, compose three short Latin sentences of your own (even simple ones like Vita est brevis). The act of composing locks in the endings.
  2. Translate Si vis pacem, para bellum and Alea iacta est using the vocabulary plus the hints in this lesson (vis = you want, para = prepare). Do the literal pass first, then the idiom.
  3. Memorize Veni, vidi, vici and say it aloud with Classical pronunciation. Then memorize one more phrase cold — recitation is the Roman method, and it still works.
Check
Translate Amor fati and name one context where a Stoic would use it.
"Love of one's fate" — used for events outside your control: instead of merely accepting them, you actively affirm them as the material you're given to work with.
Check
What does carpe literally mean, and why does it matter?
"Pluck," as in harvesting ripe fruit. It matters because the metaphor is perishability — the day ripens and rots — which "seize" alone does not convey.
Check
Translate Sapere aude and name the philosopher who made it famous.
"Dare to be wise." Kant used it as the motto of the Enlightenment — the call to think for yourself rather than follow authority.

Summary

You can now read real Latin phrases in the wild — mottos, inscriptions, state seals — by doing two passes: literal translation first, then the idiom. Reading aloud with Classical pronunciation, the way Romans learned, turns the phrases from decoration into something you actually own.

Capstone: parse and translate

▾

Objectives

  • Demonstrate the course's two core skills on fresh material: parsing English prose to its skeletons, and translating Latin phrases literally and idiomatically.
  • Produce a one-page written analysis of a real paragraph.
  • Explain what each Latin motto means beyond its literal words.

The task

Two tasks. Take your time — this is the whole course in miniature.

  1. Parse: choose any paragraph of serious prose (an essay, a speech, a contract). Reduce every sentence to its skeleton and write down exactly what each sentence claims. One page, handwritten or typed.
  2. Translate: render these into English, then explain what each means beyond the literal words: Festina lente (Augustus's motto), Dum spiro spero, Audentes fortuna iuvat.

Answers: "Make haste slowly" · "While I breathe, I hope" · "Fortune favors the bold."

How to do the parse

Pick a paragraph of three to five sentences — an op-ed, a terms-of-service clause, a speech. Number the sentences. For each one, write the skeleton (subject, verb, object) and then one line stating the claim in your own words. Worked example on a real sentence: "Our company, committed to innovation and customer satisfaction, will be launching a groundbreaking new platform next quarter." Skeleton: company will be launching platform. Claim: "We will release a new product soon." Notice what the skeleton exposed: "groundbreaking," "committed to innovation and customer satisfaction" — pure fog, zero claim. A good submission reads like an x-ray: every sentence reduced, every claim stated plainly, fog labeled as fog.

Choose difficult prose on purpose. Parsing easy sentences proves nothing; the skill pays off exactly where writers are paid to obscure. Contracts and political speeches are the final exam the world gives you daily.

How to do the translate

Literal pass first, then the meaning. Worked example: Dum spiro spero. Dum = while, spiro = I breathe, spero = I hope — "While I breathe, I hope" (the motto of South Carolina). Beyond the literal: a refusal to surrender; hope lasts exactly as long as life does, and no longer. Now do the same for the other two. Festina lente — "make haste slowly" — was one of Augustus's favorite sayings, according to Suetonius: hurry, but with deliberation; speed without care is just expensive error. Audentes fortuna iuvat — "fortune favors the bold" — comes from Virgil's Aeneid (Book 10), spoken as a battle cry: luck, the line suggests, is not random but attracted to those who act.

For each motto, write two lines: the literal rendering, then what a Roman hearing it would have understood — the attitude it prescribes, the situation it fits. That second line is the difference between translating words and translating meaning.

The ancient program, in miniature

"The parts of grammar are six: trained reading, explanation of tropes, glosses, etymology, analogy, and critical judgment." — Dionysius Thrax, Art of Grammar, 2nd century BC. This capstone mirrors his program: the parse is trained reading and critical judgment; the translation is glosses and etymology; explaining what each motto means is the explanation of tropes. Two thousand years later, the drill has not changed — only the student has.

Try
  1. Warm up by parsing these very instructions: reduce each sentence of "The task" above to its skeleton. If you cannot parse the assignment, you are not ready to parse the world.
  2. Review translation: render Per aspera ad astra literally, then idiomatically (lesson 5). Check that your two passes say different things.
  3. Write a one-paragraph reflection: describe a sentence you misread recently — a text, an email, a headline — and what parsing it would have revealed. Keep it; reread it after the capstone.
Check
Who used Festina lente as a motto, and what does it mean?
Augustus — Suetonius records it among his favorite sayings. "Make haste slowly": move fast, but with deliberation.
Check
Translate Audentes fortuna iuvat and say where it comes from.
"Fortune favors the bold." Virgil's Aeneid, Book 10 — a battle cry suggesting luck follows those who act.
Check
What is the difference between translating literally and translating meaning?
The literal pass gives word-for-word (carpe = pluck); the meaning pass gives the idiom the phrase carries ("seize the day"). Do the literal pass first to stay honest, then the idiom to make it English.

Summary

The capstone compresses the course: parse real prose to its skeletons and state each claim plainly; translate three Latin mottos literally, then explain what each prescribes. Done honestly, it proves the course's thesis — that grammar is not schoolroom drill but the discipline of seeing what is actually said.
