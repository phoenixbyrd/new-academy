Quadrivium II

Geometry

The art of proof and space. Euclid's Elements was the most-used textbook in history — two thousand years of students learned to think by proving things about triangles.

1

Euclid's game

▾

Objectives

  • State Euclid's five postulates and five common notions in your own words.
  • Explain why making assumptions explicit is what makes reasoning checkable.
  • Describe how two thousand years of itching at the fifth postulate produced non-Euclidean geometry.

The rules of the game

Euclid's genius wasn't discovering geometry — much of it was already known. It was organizing it: start from a handful of agreed starting points, and derive everything else by pure logic. The starting points are the five postulates:

  1. A straight line can be drawn between any two points.
  2. A line segment can be extended indefinitely.
  3. A circle can be drawn with any center and radius.
  4. All right angles are equal.
  5. The parallel postulate: through a point not on a line, exactly one line can be drawn parallel to it.

Notice the first three are construction licenses — they say what you are allowed to draw with a straightedge and compass. Euclid's game has equipment rules, not just belief rules. Everything proved in the thirteen books of the Elements must be buildable from these moves. Alongside the postulates, Euclid wrote down five common notions — general logical rules, not just geometric ones:

  1. Things equal to the same thing are also equal to one another.
  2. If equals are added to equals, the wholes are equal.
  3. If equals are subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal.
  4. Things which coincide with one another are equal to one another.
  5. The whole is greater than the part.

And before any of that, he defined his terms — famously, and controversially. Definition 1 of Book I reads: "A point is that which has no part." Generations of students have stared at that sentence and wondered whether it says anything at all. That unease is the point of the lesson: Euclid was trying to say exactly what he meant before arguing, and even the attempt teaches you something about the limits of definitions.

Why the fifth postulate itches

The first four feel obvious. The fifth doesn't — and that itch mattered: two thousand years of trying to prove the fifth postulate from the others eventually produced non-Euclidean geometry, which turned out to describe curved spacetime. Euclid's own statement of it is famously clunky (a whole paragraph about lines meeting on the side where interior angles sum to less than two right angles); the clean version we use today — Playfair's axiom, 1795 — is the one quoted above.

Why couldn't anyone prove it? Because it isn't provable. In the 1800s, Lobachevsky and Bolyai independently built a consistent geometry where the fifth postulate is false — where through a point off a line there are infinitely many parallels. Riemann later built another where there are none (the geometry of a sphere's surface: picture "straight lines" as great circles — any two of them intersect). Neither system contradicted itself. So the fifth postulate is independent: the rest of geometry doesn't force it. Then, in 1915, Einstein's general relativity showed that the real universe runs on Riemann's curved geometry, not Euclid's flat one. The unprovable assumption turned out to be a doorway.

Primary source: Euclid sets the terms

"Let the following be postulated: to draw a straight line from any point to any point." — Euclid, Elements, Book I, Postulate 1 (translation paraphrase). Dry as dust — and that dryness is deliberate. Euclid refuses to argue about what a line is in any deep sense; he only licenses the moves you may make with one. Modern mathematics still works this way: a group, a vector space, a topology is defined by the rules it obeys, not by what it "is." Euclid was doing axiomatic mathematics twenty-three centuries before the term existed.

The lesson: audit your fifth postulates

The lesson: in any argument, notice which premises are load-bearing and which are merely assumed. Disputes are usually about the fifth postulate — the assumption nobody examined. Euclid's discipline is a habit you can run anywhere: what am I taking for granted here, and what would change if I dropped it? Most arguments improve dramatically the moment both sides write down their postulates. The ones that don't improve were never about the evidence — they were about an unexamined fifth.

Try
  1. Read the five postulates aloud. Which feels least obvious? (It's the fifth — the parallel one.) Now ask: what "fifth postulate" am I assuming in an argument I'm currently having — the premise I haven't examined?
  2. Write the five common notions in your own words without looking. Which one gets used every time you balance a budget or check a receipt?
  3. Find one triangle on a globe — say, from the North Pole to two points on the equator 90° apart. Its angles sum to 270°, not 180°. That's non-Euclidean geometry you can hold in your head.
Check
Why did Euclid bother writing down postulates for things everyone already believed?
Because making assumptions explicit is what makes reasoning checkable. If the foundations are on the table, anyone can verify every step — and anyone can later question the foundations themselves, which is exactly what happened with the parallel postulate.
Check
What does it mean to say the parallel postulate is "independent" of the others?
It means no chain of logic from the other four (plus the definitions and common notions) can ever produce it — because consistent geometries exist where the others hold and it fails. Independence was proven by exhibiting those alternative geometries, not by failing to find a proof.
Check
Why did Euclid's Definition 1 ("a point is that which has no part") bother so many readers?
Because it defines "point" using the equally undefined idea of a "part" — it feels circular. But Euclid's real foundation isn't the definitions; it's the postulates, which license constructions. The definitions set vocabulary; the postulates set the rules of the game.

Summary

Euclid turned geometry into a game with stated rules — postulates for construction, common notions for logic — and showed that everything follows from them. The one rule that couldn't be proved from the rest opened the door to non-Euclidean geometry and, eventually, to curved spacetime. The habit to keep: write your assumptions down, so you can question them later.

2

Triangles and Pythagoras

▾

Objectives

  • State the Pythagorean theorem and its converse precisely.
  • Reproduce the rearrangement proof (tilted square) from memory.
  • Use the theorem to compute unknown sides, including the 3-4-5 family.
  • Explain why the angles of any triangle sum to 180°.

The atom of shape

The triangle is geometry's atom: any polygon can be cut into triangles, so triangle facts become all shape facts. A quadrilateral splits into two triangles along a diagonal; a pentagon into three; an n-sided polygon into n−2. Prove something about triangles and you've proved it about every flat shape there is.

The first great triangle fact: its three interior angles always sum to 180° — two right angles, as Euclid puts it in Proposition I.32. The proof is one clever construction. Picture triangle ABC. Through vertex C, draw a line parallel to the base AB (the parallel postulate earns its keep here). The new line forms two "alternate interior" angles with the triangle's sides — and alternate interior angles made by a parallel cut are equal. So the three angles at vertex C, sitting on a straight line, are exactly the triangle's three angles: they sum to 180°. One parallel line, and the whole triangle is measured.

The second fact: area. A rectangle of length l and width w has area l×w — take that as the ground floor. Cut the rectangle along a diagonal and you get two identical triangles, each exactly half: area of a triangle = ½ × base × height. The height must be measured perpendicular to the base — drop the altitude, the shortest distance from the opposite vertex to the base line.

Pythagoras' theorem

The king of triangle facts: a² + b² = c² for a right triangle — the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides. The Babylonians knew it a thousand years before Pythagoras; the Greeks proved it.

A proof you can see: draw a square of side (a+b). Inside it, place four copies of the right triangle, one in each corner, tilted inward. They enclose a tilted inner square of side c. The big square's area is (a+b)²; it's also 4 triangles (4 × ab/2 = 2ab) plus c². So (a+b)² = 2ab + c² → a² + 2ab + b² = 2ab + c² → a² + b² = c². No algebra required to see it.

A worked example: legs 3 and 4. Picture a right triangle with legs 3 and 4. The squares on the legs have areas 9 and 16; their sum is 25; so the hypotenuse squared is 25 and the hypotenuse is 5. Check: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5². The theorem also runs backward — its converse: if a triangle's sides satisfy a² + b² = c², then the angle opposite side c is a right angle. That converse is what makes the 3-4-5 rope trick below work, and it's how builders have squared corners for millennia.

Another worked example, less friendly numbers: legs 5 and 12. 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13², so the hypotenuse is 13. Triples like (3,4,5), (5,12,13), and (8,15,17) are worth memorizing — they recur constantly. And note the pattern: scaling a triple scales the triangle, so (6,8,10) is right too, just a doubled 3-4-5.

Primary source: Euclid I.47

"In right-angled triangles the square on the side opposite the right angle equals the sum of the squares on the sides containing the right angle." — Euclid, Elements, Book I, Proposition 47. Euclid's own proof is famously elaborate — he builds squares outward on all three sides and shows, by a chain of congruent triangles, that the two smaller squares exactly fill the large one. It's longer than the rearrangement proof above, but it uses nothing beyond what Books I has already established. The theorem sits at position 47 of 48 propositions in Book I: nearly the climax of the whole book.

Modern use: distance itself

Pythagoras is hiding inside the modern formula for distance. On a map grid, the distance between (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) is √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)²) — the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs (x₂−x₁) and (y₂−y₁). Every GPS, every video game, every "nearest store" search is computing a² + b² = c² millions of times a second. The theorem the Greeks proved about rope and bronze is the geometry of the digital map.

Try
  1. The 3-4-5 trick: tie knots in a rope at 3, 4, and 5 unit intervals, pull it into a triangle, and you get a perfect right angle — Egyptian surveyors and modern carpenters both use it. Test it on any corner in your home.
  2. Compute the hypotenuse for legs 5 and 12 (answer: 13). Then for legs 8 and 15 (answer: 17). Do the arithmetic by hand once so the pattern sticks.
  3. Is a triangle with sides 6, 8, 10 right-angled? Use the converse: 6² + 8² = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10². Yes. Now try 5, 5, 8 — is that one right? (25 + 25 = 50 ≠ 64, so no.)
Check
Why is the visual proof above a proof, not just a picture?
Because it works for any a and b — the algebra shows the relationship holds regardless of the triangle's size. A single measured example would be evidence; the general argument is proof.
Check
A ladder 10 feet long leans against a wall with its foot 6 feet out. How high does it reach?
The ladder is the hypotenuse (c = 10), the ground distance is one leg (a = 6). So 6² + b² = 10² → 36 + b² = 100 → b² = 64 → b = 8 feet.
Check
Where does the parallel postulate enter the proof that a triangle's angles sum to 180°?
In drawing the line through vertex C parallel to the base — the construction assumes exactly one such parallel exists — and in the claim that alternate interior angles are equal, which is equivalent to the parallel postulate.

Summary

Triangles are the atoms of flat shapes: their angles sum to 180°, their area is ½ × base × height, and right triangles obey a² + b² = c² — provable by a picture that works for every size, with a converse that lets you test for right angles. The theorem lives on inside every distance calculation your phone performs.

Circles and pi

▾

Objectives

  • Define π as the ratio of circumference to diameter and explain why it is constant.
  • Describe Archimedes' polygon-squeeze method and his bounds for π.
  • Use circumference = 2πr and area = πr², and explain the wedge intuition for the area formula.

One number for every circle

Measure any circle — a coin, a wheel, a plate — and divide its circumference by its diameter. You always get the same number: π ≈ 3.14159… That constancy is a genuine wonder: every circle in the universe obeys the same ratio. Why? Because all circles are similar (lesson 5's territory): scale any circle up or down and every length scales by the same factor, so the ratio of two lengths — circumference to diameter — can't change. π is the fingerprint of circularity itself.

Formally: π is defined as C/d, circumference divided by diameter. Since d = 2r, the circumference formula follows immediately: C = 2πr. The diameter is the natural unit because it's the circle's full width — the thing you'd actually measure across a wheel.

Archimedes' squeeze

Archimedes pinned it down by squeezing the circle between inscribed and circumscribed polygons — more sides, tighter squeeze — bounding π between 3.1408 and 3.1429 over two thousand years ago, by hand.

Here's the method in detail. Picture a circle with a hexagon drawn inside it, vertices touching the circle. The hexagon's perimeter is shorter than the circle's circumference — the straight edges cut corners. Now draw a hexagon outside the circle, sides tangent to it; its perimeter is longer than the circumference. So: inner perimeter < circumference < outer perimeter. Double the sides to 12, then 24, 48, 96 — each doubling hugs the circle tighter, and the gap between the two bounds shrinks. Archimedes carried this to 96-sided polygons, computing perimeters with nothing but square roots done by hand, and proved:

3 + 10/71 < π < 3 + 1/7

That's 3.1408… < π < 3.1428… — accurate to two decimal places, in the 3rd century BC. (His treatise Measurement of a Circle also gives the famous area result: a circle's area equals a right triangle with legs r and C — i.e., ½rC = πr².) The upper bound, 22/7 = 3.142857…, is the handy approximation still taught in schools; Archimedes himself knew it was slightly too big.

"The ratio of the circumference of any circle to its diameter is less than 3 1/7 but greater than 3 10/71." — Archimedes, Measurement of a Circle, Proposition 3. What stuns isn't the digits — it's the method: trap the unknown between two knowns and squeeze. That idea, refined over centuries, became the limit — the engine of calculus.

From circumference to area

The practical formulas: circumference = 2πr, area = πr². And the area formula has a beautiful intuition: slice a circle into many thin wedges and rearrange them into a near-rectangle of height r and width half the circumference (πr) — area = r × πr = πr².

Make it concrete. Picture a pizza cut into 8 wedges, then re-laid point-to-tip, alternating up and down, like a zigzag strip. The strip's top and bottom edges are bumpy arcs, but its height is the radius r and its total length is half the circumference, πr. Now cut into 16 wedges, 32, 64 — the bumps flatten out and the strip becomes a true rectangle: height r, width πr, area πr². Archimedes proved this rigorously with his squeeze; the pizza picture is the intuition.

A worked example: a circle of radius 7. Circumference = 2π × 7 = 14π ≈ 43.98. Area = π × 7² = 49π ≈ 153.94. And a scaling check that foreshadows next lesson: double the radius to 14 and the area becomes π × 196 = 196π — four times bigger, not twice.

The unending decimals

π is irrational (its decimals never repeat) and transcendental (it can't be the solution to any finite polynomial equation) — proven, not guessed. Irrationality was proved by Lambert in 1761; transcendence by Lindemann in 1882. The transcendence proof settled a 2,000-year-old question: squaring the circle — constructing a square with the same area as a given circle using only straightedge and compass — is impossible, because it would require constructing π, and transcendental numbers can't be built that way.

Try
  1. Measure any round object: wrap a string around it (circumference), measure across it (diameter), divide. You'll get ~3.14 every time. Do three objects and feel the constancy.
  2. A circular garden has radius 7 meters. Compute its area by hand using π ≈ 22/7: area = (22/7) × 49 = 22 × 7 = 154 m². (That's why 22/7 is beloved — it makes multiples of 7 easy.)
  3. Archimedes' bounds: verify that 3 + 10/71 ≈ 3.1408 by dividing 10 by 71 (you'll get 0.1408…), and that 3 + 1/7 ≈ 3.1429. True π, 3.14159…, sits between them. He trapped it with 96-gons and hand arithmetic.
Check
What was Archimedes' key idea for bounding π?
Squeeze the circle between two polygons — one inside, one outside. As the number of sides grows, both polygons' perimeters close in on the circumference, trapping π in an ever-narrower interval.
Check
If a circle's diameter doubles, what happens to its circumference and its area?
Circumference doubles (C = πd is linear in d). Area quadruples: A = πr², and doubling the diameter doubles the radius, so the area multiplies by 2² = 4.
Check
Why must every circle in the universe share the same circumference-to-diameter ratio?
Because any two circles are similar — one is a scaled copy of the other. Scaling multiplies every length by the same factor, so the ratio of any two corresponding lengths is unchanged. The ratio is a property of circularity, not of size.

Summary

π is the constant ratio of circumference to diameter, true for every circle because all circles are similar. Archimedes trapped it between 3 + 10/71 and 3 + 1/7 by squeezing the circle between polygons — the ancestor of the limit. Circumference = 2πr, area = πr² (seen in the wedge rearrangement), and π's decimals never repeat and never satisfy any polynomial — proven facts, not mysteries.

Area, volume, and the tyranny of scale

▾

Objectives

  • Build area and volume formulas from the rectangle upward, and use the standard formula table fluently.
  • State Archimedes' sphere results (volume ⁴⁄₃πr³, surface 4πr²) and the 2:3 cylinder relationship.
  • Apply the square-cube scaling law to explain why size changes what is possible.

Building formulas from the rectangle

Formulas are easier to keep if you build them: a rectangle's area (length × width) cut diagonally gives two triangles — so triangle area is half the rectangle's. A circle rearranged into wedges gives πr² (last lesson). Stack flat shapes and you get volume: prism volume = base area × height. A sphere's volume (⁴⁄₃πr³) and surface area (4πr²) were Archimedes' proudest results — he had them carved on his tomb.

The build-up is systematic. A parallelogram is a slanted rectangle: shear it upright (same base, same height) and the area doesn't change — base × height. A triangle is half a parallelogram — ½ × base × height. A trapezoid with parallel sides a and b and height h is the average: ½(a+b) × h (two copies make a parallelogram with base a+b). Each formula is earned from the one before, not memorized from a poster.

Volume adds one move: extrude. Drag any flat shape straight up through height h and you get a prism whose volume is base area × height — a box (l×w×h), a cylinder (πr²h), anything. A pyramid or cone tapers to a point, and tapering costs exactly two-thirds: volume = ⅓ × base area × height. (Why a third? Three pyramids exactly fill a cube — a fact you can verify with a 3D-printed dissection.) Reference table:

Triangle½ × b × hCylinderπr²h

Trapezoid½(a+b) × hSphere⁴⁄₃πr³

Worked example: a sphere of radius 3. Volume = ⁴⁄₃π × 27 = 36π ≈ 113.1. Surface area = 4π × 9 = 36π ≈ 113.1 — the same number, a coincidence of r = 3 (volume grows as r³, surface as r², and they cross at r = 3). For r = 1: volume ⁴⁄₃π ≈ 4.19, surface 4π ≈ 12.57.

Primary source: Archimedes' tomb

"Any sphere is equal to four times the cone whose base is the great circle of the sphere and whose height is the radius" — and, the result he loved most: a sphere's volume and surface area are each exactly two-thirds of its circumscribing cylinder's. Picture a ball snug inside a can: the ball fills ⅔ of the can's volume, and the ball's surface equals ⅔ of the can's total surface. Archimedes proved this in On the Sphere and Cylinder, and by his own wish the sphere-in-cylinder was carved on his tombstone — Cicero later found the overgrown grave by it. A mathematician's proudest theorem as a headstone: there is no better summary of what this subject meant to him.

The tyranny of scale

The crucial modern idea is scaling: double a shape's edge and its area quadruples (2²) while its volume octuples (2³). This is why ants can't be human-sized (their legs, scaled up, couldn't carry their cubed weight), why large animals need disproportionately thick bones, and why a 16-inch pizza is four times the 8-inch — not twice. The pizza fact alone will save you money for life.

Galileo nailed this in 1638 in Two New Sciences: the square-cube law. Strength depends on cross-sectional area (∝ L²); weight depends on volume (∝ L³). Scale any animal up by 10 and its bones are 100× stronger but its body 1,000× heavier — relative strength collapses tenfold. That's why elephants have columnar legs and shrews have twigs, why a child can fall off a jungle gym and an adult breaks a hip from the same relative fall, and why giant movie monsters are physically impossible: King Kong's femurs would snap under his own cubed weight.

Run the numbers on the pizza: 8-inch pizza has radius 4, area = 16π ≈ 50.3 in². 16-inch has radius 8, area = 64π ≈ 201.1 in² — exactly four times. If the 16-inch costs less than four times the 8-inch (it almost always does), it's the better deal per square inch. Scaling beats intuition; do the powers.

Try
  1. Answer from scaling, not formulas: why does doubling a cube's edge multiply its volume by 8? (Hint: how many original cubes fit along each edge of the doubled cube?) Then verify the pizza claim with πr².
  2. A sphere of radius 2 has volume ⁴⁄₃π × 8 = ³²⁄₃π ≈ 33.5. Double the radius to 4 and compute the new volume (⁴⁄₃π × 64 = ²⁵⁶⁄₃π ≈ 268.1). Confirm it's exactly 8× — volume scales as the cube.
  3. Scale argument: a 1:10 model of a bridge. Its members' cross-sections are (1/10)² = 1/100 as strong, but its weight is (1/10)³ = 1/1000. So the model is 10× stronger relative to its weight — which is why a model that stands fine can collapse at full size. Prototypes deceive; do the powers.
Check
A model is built at 1:10 scale. How much paint (area) and how much material (volume) does it need relative to the real thing?
Paint: (1/10)² = 1/100 of the area. Material: (1/10)³ = 1/1000 of the volume. Scale the linear dimension and the rest follows by powers — this is why models are cheap and prototypes deceive.
Check
Why is a pyramid's volume ⅓ × base × height, and how could you convince yourself physically?
Three square pyramids with the cube's base and half its height exactly fill the cube — you can verify with three identical 3D-printed or cardboard pyramids. The ⅓ is not a convention; it's what the dissection shows. The same factor holds for cones versus cylinders.
Check
A 12-inch pizza costs $14 and an 8-inch costs $8. Which is the better deal per square inch?
12-inch: radius 6, area 36π ≈ 113.1 in² → $14/113.1 ≈ 12.4¢/in². 8-inch: radius 4, area 16π ≈ 50.3 in² → $8/50.3 ≈ 15.9¢/in². The 12-inch wins — area scales as the square, so the bigger pizza is almost always cheaper per bite.

Summary

Every area formula grows out of the rectangle and every volume out of extrusion, with the sphere's ⁴⁄₃πr³ and 4πr² as Archimedes' crown jewels — each ⅔ of the circumscribing cylinder, carved on his tomb. And the square-cube law governs everything big and small: scale length by L and area follows L², volume L³ — which is why giants need tree-trunk legs and the 16-inch pizza is four times the 8-inch.

5

Similarity and scale: measuring the unreachable

▾

Objectives

  • Define similarity: equal angles, all corresponding lengths in one common ratio.
  • Use the AA (angle-angle) criterion to establish similarity.
  • Measure unreachable heights and distances with shadows and scale ratios.

Same shape, different size

Two shapes are similar when they have the same shape but possibly different size: all corresponding angles equal, and all corresponding lengths in the same ratio. A photograph and its enlargement are similar; so are a floor plan and the building, a map and the terrain. Similarity is the geometry of copies.

For triangles there is a beautifully cheap test: AA — if two angles match, the triangles are similar. The third angle must match automatically, because all three sum to 180° (lesson 2): if two are fixed, the third is 180° minus their sum. And once the angles match, the sides are forced into proportion — Euclid proves this as Proposition VI.4. So to show two triangles are scaled copies, you only ever need two angles.

Thales' intercept theorem is the workhorse version: draw a line parallel to one side of a triangle, cutting the other two sides, and it divides those sides proportionally. Picture triangle ABC with a line through points D (on AB) and E (on AC), parallel to BC. Then AD/DB = AE/EC, and the small triangle ADE is similar to the whole ABC. Every shadow measurement below is this theorem in disguise.

Thales and the pyramid

Legend says Thales measured the Great Pyramid's height by waiting until his own shadow equaled his height — at that moment, the pyramid's shadow equaled the pyramid's height. The principle is similarity: same shape, different size, and all corresponding lengths in the same ratio. If a 6-foot person casts a 4-foot shadow while a tree casts a 40-foot shadow, the tree is 60 feet tall — no climbing required.

Work it as a proportion. You and your shadow form a right triangle; the tree and its shadow form another. The sun's rays arrive parallel (the sun is effectively infinitely far away), so both triangles share the sun's angle above the horizon — two angles match (the sun angle and the right angle at the ground), so by AA the triangles are similar. Therefore:

your height / your shadow = tree height / tree shadow

With numbers: 6 ft / 4 ft = h / 40 ft → h = 40 × (6/4) = 60 ft. Thales' elegant special case: wait until your shadow equals your height (ratio 1:1), and the pyramid's height simply equals its shadow's length — no arithmetic at all. The catch he had to handle: a pyramid's shadow must be measured from the center of its base, not its edge, so he added half the base's width. Details matter in fieldwork.

Maps, models, and perspective

Similarity is the quiet engine of maps, models, photography, and perspective drawing — every map is a similarity transformation of the land, and every scale on it is a ratio you can compute with. The Greeks used it for surveying, astronomy, and architecture; you use it every time you read "1 inch = 1 mile."

Read a scale bar as a proportion. A 1:50,000 map means 1 unit on paper = 50,000 units on land. Measure 4 cm between two towns: real distance = 4 cm × 50,000 = 200,000 cm = 2,000 m = 2 km. "1 inch = 1 mile" is the ratio 1:63,360 (a mile is 63,360 inches). Renaissance painters used the same mathematics in reverse — linear perspective is the projection of a 3D scene onto a 2D canvas along lines through the eye, and similar triangles guarantee that distant objects shrink in exact proportion. Every photograph you've ever taken is a similarity machine.

Try
  1. Measure your own height and your shadow's length at the same moment you measure a tree's or building's shadow. Compute the height from the ratio. Check against a known height if you can — you'll be within a few percent.
  2. A 6-foot person casts a 4-foot shadow; at the same moment a flagpole casts a 30-foot shadow. Set up the proportion (6/4 = h/30) and solve: h = 45 feet.
  3. On a 1:24,000 map (1 inch = 2,000 feet), two trailheads are 3.5 inches apart. Real distance = 3.5 × 2,000 = 7,000 feet ≈ 1.33 miles.
Check
Why do similar triangles guarantee the shadow method works?
Because the sun's rays hit the ground at the same angle everywhere nearby, the triangle formed by you and your shadow has the same angles as the triangle formed by the tree and its shadow. Same angles → same shape → all lengths in the same ratio.
Check
Why is it enough to check two angles (AA) to prove two triangles similar?
Because every triangle's angles sum to 180° — fixing two fixes the third (it's 180° minus the other two). With all three angles matching, Euclid VI.4 forces the sides into a common proportion.
Check
A model car is 1:18 scale and 25 cm long. How long is the real car?
25 cm × 18 = 450 cm = 4.5 meters — about 14.8 feet. Scale ratios multiply straight through: one measurement on the model gives every corresponding real measurement.

Summary

Similar figures share angles and scale all lengths by one ratio — and for triangles, two matching angles (AA) are enough to prove it. That single fact lets you measure pyramids with shadows, land with maps, and scenes with cameras: find the proportion, multiply through, and the unreachable becomes measurable.

Capstone: Eratosthenes-style measurement

▾

Objectives

  • Reconstruct Eratosthenes' reasoning: shadow angle → fraction of a circle → Earth's circumference.
  • Measure a tall object by the shadow method and estimate your error honestly.
  • Reproduce the tilted-square proof of Pythagoras from memory, with every step labeled.

Eratosthenes' audacity

Around 240 BC, Eratosthenes measured the Earth's circumference with a stick, a well, and geometry: at noon on the summer solstice the sun shone straight down a well in Syene, while in Alexandria a stick cast a shadow at 7.2° — one-fiftieth of a circle. So Alexandria was one-fiftieth of the Earth's circumference from Syene. He was within a few percent.

Reconstructed from later writers like Cleomedes (his own account is lost): Eratosthenes was chief librarian at Alexandria. He heard that at Syene — modern Aswan, in southern Egypt — on the summer solstice at noon, the sun stood directly overhead: vertical sticks cast no shadow, and sunlight reached the bottom of a deep well. In Alexandria, nearly due north, a vertical stick did cast a shadow at that same moment. He measured the shadow's angle: about 7.2°, which is 1/50 of a full 360° circle (7.2/360 = 1/50 — check it). The overland distance between the cities was reckoned at about 5,000 stadia. So the full circumference: 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia.

How good was that? Honestly: it depends on the stadion, and scholars still argue about which one he meant. If he used the Egyptian stadion of about 157.5 meters, 250,000 stadia ≈ 39,375 km — within about 2% of the true 40,075 km, a staggering achievement. If he used the longer Olympic stadion (~185 m), the result overshoots by ~15%. The honest statement: his method was essentially perfect and his result was within a few percent on the most plausible unit — with the unit itself as the main uncertainty. Report numbers with the error bars attached.

The geometry behind it

Why does the shadow angle equal the fraction of the Earth's circle? Picture the Earth as a circle (a cross-section through both cities and the poles). The sun's rays arrive effectively parallel — the sun is ~150 million km away, so rays hitting Syene and Alexandria are parallel to exquisite precision. At Syene the ray runs straight down the well: it points at Earth's center. At Alexandria the ray makes a 7.2° angle with the vertical stick. Now extend both verticals down to Earth's center: they meet there, and the angle between them is the shadow angle — 7.2° — because the stick's vertical and the sun's ray are cut by the same parallel-line geometry (alternate interior angles again, the old friend from lesson 2). So Alexandria sits 7.2° of arc from Syene along the meridian: 1/50 of the circle. One angle, one distance, and the whole planet is measured. Note the assumptions doing quiet work: Earth is spherical, the cities share a meridian (they nearly do), and the sun's rays are parallel. Eratosthenes got all three right.

Your mission

Your version, two tasks:

  1. Measure something big with something small: use the shadow method (lesson 5) to measure the tallest thing near you, and document your method, numbers, and estimated error.
  2. Draw your own proof of Pythagoras: on paper, reproduce the tilted-square proof from lesson 2 from memory, labeling every step. If you can reconstruct it without looking, you don't just know the theorem — you own it.

For task 1, do it like a field report. Pick a sunny moment and work fast — shadows move. Record: your height, your shadow's length, the object's shadow length, the time, and which end of the object's shadow you measured from (for a tree, the trunk's base; estimate if the canopy overhangs). Compute the height from the proportion, then estimate your error: how precisely could you read each shadow (± a few inches?), and how much would that shift the answer? You have a tape measure — use it twice.

For task 2, the bar is reconstruction, not copying. On blank paper, draw: the big (a+b) square, the four right triangles in the corners, the tilted inner square of side c. Label the areas — (a+b)² for the whole, 2ab for the four triangles, c² for the inner square — and write the algebra that cancels to a² + b² = c². If you stall, note exactly where, peek once, and try again tomorrow. The stalled spot is where the learning lives.

Why it matters

Eratosthenes didn't just measure the Earth — he showed that the method of geometry reaches past the horizon. The same similar-triangles reasoning that sizes up a tree (lesson 5) sized up the planet; the same postulate-auditing habit (lesson 1) let him list his assumptions. Two millennia later, surveyors still triangulate the same way, and GPS still solves the same triangle — with satellites instead of shadows. The capstone's real lesson: mathematics is not about the objects on your desk. It's about the reach of the reasoning.

Try
  1. Verify Eratosthenes' arithmetic: 7.2/360 simplifies to 1/50 (divide top and bottom by 7.2), and 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia. Then convert: at 157.5 m per stadion, that's 39,375 km. Compare with the true 40,075 km and compute the percent error (≈ 1.7%).
  2. Suppose his shadow angle had been 7.5° instead of 7.2°. What circumference would he have computed? (7.5/360 = 1/48, so 48 × 5,000 = 240,000 stadia.) A 0.3° error moves the answer by 10,000 stadia — feel how sensitive the method is to the angle.

Why must the sun's rays be treated as parallel for Eratosthenes' method to work?
Because the whole argument identifies the shadow angle at Alexandria with the central angle at Earth's center between the two cities. That identification uses alternate-interior-angles geometry, which requires the two sun rays to be parallel — true to exquisite precision since the sun is ~150 million km away.

Summary

With one shadow angle (7.2° = 1/50 of a circle) and one surveyed distance (~5,000 stadia), Eratosthenes computed Earth's circumference as ~250,000 stadia — within a few percent on the most plausible stadion, with the unit as the honest uncertainty.
