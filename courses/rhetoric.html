Trivium III

Rhetoric

The art of speaking well. Logic finds the truth; rhetoric carries it to other minds intact.

1

The five canons

▾

Classical rhetoric had five canons — five jobs every speaker must do:

  1. Invention (inventio): finding what to say — discovering arguments.
  2. Arrangement (dispositio): ordering it for maximum clarity and force.
  3. Style (elocutio): choosing words well.
  4. Memory (memoria): internalizing the speech.
  5. Delivery (actio): voice, gesture, presence.

Notice: three canons are about thinking, two about performing. Modern "public speaking advice" obsesses over delivery and skips invention — which is why so many confident speakers have nothing to say.

Try
Think of the last persuasive thing you said (an argument, a request, a pitch). Which canon did you actually do well, and which did you skip?
Check
Why is rhetoric not manipulation?
Because rhetoric is the art of effective communication, not deceptive communication. Logic constrains what you may honestly claim; rhetoric concerns how truthfully-claimed things are best conveyed. Using these tools to mislead is a failure of the speaker's ethics, not of the art.
2

Invention: finding arguments

▾

The Greeks didn't wait for inspiration; they had topics (topoi) — places to look for arguments. Run any position through these:

  • Definition: what exactly are we talking about? (Half of all disputes are definition disputes.)
  • Comparison: what is this like? What is it unlike?
  • Cause and effect: what produces it? What does it produce?
  • Testimony: who has seen this? What do records show?
  • Consequences: if we act, what follows? If we don't?

Stasis theory — the master tool. Before arguing, establish what kind of disagreement this is: conjecture (did it happen?), definition (what do we call it?), quality (was it justified?), policy (what should we do?). Most doomed arguments are people answering different stasis questions past each other.

Try
Take a disagreement you're currently in (or observing). Identify its stasis. Then run your position through the five topics and write down the two strongest arguments you hadn't thought of.
Check
Two people argue about a new law. One cites crime statistics, the other says "it's just wrong." What happened?
They're at different stasis points — one argues consequences (policy/quality), the other argues moral definition. Until they agree on the question, no argument can land.
3

Arrangement: the classical speech

▾

Cicero's six-part structure still underlies every effective persuasive piece — essays, videos, pitches:

  1. Exordium — the opening: win attention and goodwill. Start with the stakes, not throat-clearing.
  2. Narratio — the facts: a brief, neutral account of what happened. No argument yet.
  3. Partitio — the roadmap: "Here is what I'll show, in this order." (Audiences relax when they know the shape.)
  4. Confirmatio — your case: arguments in ascending order of strength.
  5. Refutatio — answer the objections before the audience raises them. This is where credibility is won.
  6. Peroratio — the close: restate, amplify, and tell them what to do or feel now.
Try
Outline a 3-minute talk (a request to a boss, a toast, a complaint that needs fixing) using all six parts. Keep the whole outline under 150 words.
Check
Why put your strongest argument last in the confirmatio rather than first?
Recency and momentum: the audience carries your strongest point into the refutatio and peroratio. Leading with it spends your best ammunition before the audience is warmed up — and weakens the close.
4

Style: clarity is the virtue

▾

The ancients named three levels of style — plain (teach), middle (please), grand (move) — and the master knew when each was due. For almost all modern purposes, the plain style wins. Its rules:

  • Concrete over abstract: "the 2018 van" beats "the vehicle asset."
  • Short sentences for punch, long for flow — vary deliberately.
  • Active voice unless the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.
  • Cut throat-clearing: "It is important to note that" → delete.

A few schemes (patterns) that actually earn their keep: tricolon (three parallel phrases — "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness"), anaphora (repetition at the start — "we shall fight… we shall fight…"), antithesis ("not X but Y"). Use sparingly; a speech full of devices is a fireworks show, not an argument.

Try
Rewrite this in plain style: "It is imperative that we leverage our core competencies to operationalize a paradigm shift in customer-facing deliverables." Then rewrite it once more in the grand style — and notice which one you'd actually trust.
Check
When is the grand style appropriate?
When the audience must be moved to costly action — eulogies, crisis speeches, calls to sacrifice. Using it for routine matters sounds pompous and destroys trust.
5

Ethos, pathos, logos — in that order

▾

Aristotle's three appeals are usually listed logos-first. In practice, audiences process them in reverse order of prestige:

  • Ethos (character): Should I trust this person? Decided in the first minute — from honesty signals, admitted limits, and visible fairness (including steelmanning opponents).
  • Pathos (emotion): Do I care? Stories, concrete particulars, and stakes move people; abstractions don't.
  • Logos (reason): Does it hold up? The argument itself — which only gets a hearing after ethos and pathos open the door.

The ethical line: pathos in service of a true logos is persuasion. Pathos as a substitute for logos is manipulation. The test is whether the emotional appeal would survive the audience learning the full facts.

Try
Recall a speech, ad, or video that moved you. Identify one ethos move, one pathos move, and the actual logos underneath. Was the logos solid, or was it decoration?
Check
What's the fastest way to build ethos with a skeptical audience?
Concede something real: admit a limit, grant the opponent's strongest point, or disclose an interest. Costly honesty is the most credible signal there is.
6

Memory and delivery: the method of loci

▾

Roman orators spoke for hours without notes using the method of loci: associate each point with a location along a familiar route (your house, your walk to work), in order. To recall, mentally walk the route. It works because human memory is spatial first, verbal second — modern memory champions still use exactly this.

Delivery basics (actio): speak slightly slower than feels natural; pause after important sentences (silence is punctuation); plant your feet; gesture from the shoulder, not the wrist; look at one person at a time. Nerves are fuel — the goal isn't calm, it's controlled energy.

Try
Memorize a 10-item grocery list with the method of loci: place each item somewhere along your walk through your home. Test yourself an hour later, then tomorrow.
Check
Why does the method of loci work better than rote repetition?
It piggybacks on the brain's ancient spatial-navigation system, which is far stronger than its verbal rehearsal loop — and it adds vivid, distinctive, ordered retrieval cues instead of one fragile chain.
★

Capstone: the three-minute speech

▾

Write and deliver (aloud, to a person or a camera) a three-minute persuasive speech:

  1. Use the six-part arrangement.
  2. Include a refutatio — answer your strongest objection honestly.
  3. One tricolon or anaphora, no more.
  4. Memorize it with the method of loci — no notes.

Then evaluate: did you persuade, or merely perform? Ask your listener what they remember tomorrow. What survives 24 hours is what worked.
