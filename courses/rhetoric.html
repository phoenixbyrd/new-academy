Trivium III

Rhetoric

The art of speaking well. Logic finds the truth; rhetoric carries it to other minds intact.

1

The five canons

▾

Objectives

  • Name the five canons in English and Latin, and say what each one does.
  • Explain why the canons are ordered thinking-first, performing-second.
  • Diagnose a weak talk or essay by which canon broke down.

Classical rhetoric had five canons — five jobs every speaker must do:

  1. Invention (inventio): finding what to say — discovering arguments.
  2. Arrangement (dispositio): ordering it for maximum clarity and force.
  3. Style (elocutio): choosing words well.
  4. Memory (memoria): internalizing the speech.
  5. Delivery (actio): voice, gesture, presence.

The three canons of thinking

Invention is not creativity — it is discovery. The Latin inventio comes from invenire, "to find": the arguments are treated as already existing, waiting to be located, the way a prospector finds gold rather than manufacturing it. Suppose you need your landlord to fix the heat. Invention asks: what can honestly be said here? The lease clause. The cost of burst pipes versus a repair. The fact that three other tenants have the same problem. Arrangement then orders those findings for maximum force — lead with the shared problem, close with the pipe-burst math. Style words them: not "it is imperative that thermal remediation be effectuated" but "the heat has been out for six days."

The Roman teacher Quintilian defined the whole art this way:

"Rhetoric is the science of speaking well" — bene dicendi scientia. — Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria

The context matters: for Quintilian, well is a moral word, not a technical one. His ideal orator is vir bonus dicendi peritus — "a good man skilled in speaking." Skill without character is not rhetoric done well; it is rhetoric done dangerously. Keep that definition in mind through all six lessons: the art includes its ethics.

The two canons of performing

Memory means internalizing the speech, not memorizing it word for word. A memorized script shatters the moment you lose your place; an internalized speech bends with the room — you can skip, expand, or answer a question and find your way back. Delivery (actio) is voice, gesture, timing, and presence: the physical channel the argument travels through.

Cicero, speaking through his character Crassus in De Oratore, gave delivery the highest possible praise:

"Delivery, I say, has the sole and supreme power in oratory; without it, a speaker of the highest mental capacity can be held in no esteem." — Cicero, De Oratore III

The context: Cicero is not contradicting the thinking-first order. He is warning that the channel matters too — the best argument in the world, mumbled and rushed, dies on arrival. Delivery is supreme among equals, not a substitute for having something to say.

The order is the point

Notice: three canons are about thinking, two about performing. Modern "public speaking advice" obsesses over delivery and skips invention — which is why so many confident speakers have nothing to say. The canons are ordered the way a builder orders a house: you do not pick paint before you pour the foundation. Here is each canon as a question, with what skipping it looks like:

CanonQuestion it answersSkipping it looks like
InventionWhat do I actually have to say?Confident delivery of nothing
ArrangementIn what order?Rambling — strong points buried
StyleIn what words?Jargon that hides thin thinking
MemoryDo I own it?Reading slides, losing the thread
DeliveryHow do I land it?Monotone that kills good content

When a talk fails, diagnose it by canon. A brilliant argument that bored everyone was usually an actio failure. A lively talk that changed nobody's mind was usually an inventio failure — the speaker never found anything worth saying. The fix depends entirely on which canon broke.

Try
  1. Think of the last persuasive thing you said (an argument, a request, a pitch). Which canon did you actually do well, and which did you skip?
  2. Watch any five-minute video essay. Label each minute with the canon it is mostly doing — invention, arrangement, style, or delivery.
  3. Recall a talk that bored you. Name the single canon that failed, and say what fixing it would have changed.
Check
Why is rhetoric not manipulation?
Because rhetoric is the art of effective communication, not deceptive communication. Logic constrains what you may honestly claim; rhetoric concerns how truthfully-claimed things are best conveyed. Using these tools to mislead is a failure of the speaker's ethics, not of the art.
Check
A speaker rehearses delivery for hours but never writes down her arguments. Which canons is she training, and what will go wrong?
She is training only memory and delivery — the two performing canons. She will perform confidently, and the performance will carry nothing: invention, arrangement, and style were never done. Confidence without content.
Check
Why does Quintilian call rhetoric the "science of speaking well" rather than just "speaking persuasively"?
Because well binds skill to character. For Quintilian the ideal orator is a good person skilled in speaking — the art includes its ethics. Persuasion without goodness is not rhetoric done well.

Summary

Rhetoric has five canons — invention, arrangement, style, memory, delivery — ordered thinking-first because arguments must exist before they can be performed. Quintilian's definition reminds us the art includes its ethics, and Cicero's praise of delivery reminds us the channel matters too. Every failure of persuasion can be diagnosed by canon.

2

Invention: finding arguments

▾

Objectives

  • Run any position through the five topics (topoi) to find arguments.
  • Identify the stasis of a disagreement before arguing about it.
  • Build one enthymeme — rhetoric's syllogism — from a topic.

The Greeks didn't wait for inspiration; they had topics (topoi) — places to look for arguments. Run any position through these:

  • Definition: what exactly are we talking about? (Half of all disputes are definition disputes.)
  • Comparison: what is this like? What is it unlike?
  • Cause and effect: what produces it? What does it produce?
  • Testimony: who has seen this? What do records show?
  • Consequences: if we act, what follows? If we don't?

The topics at work

Take a position: the town should add a bus stop on Main Street. Run it through the topics and arguments appear on demand:

  • Definition: What counts as "adequate" transit? If the town's own plan promises service within a half-mile of every resident, Main Street fails the definition.
  • Comparison: The neighboring town added two stops last year and ridership rose 20%. What is our street like? What is it unlike?
  • Cause and effect: No stop causes a long walk; the long walk causes missed buses; missed buses cause lost jobs. Trace the chain.
  • Testimony: The driver logs show people flagging the bus down mid-block. The ridership survey names Main Street first.
  • Consequences: A stop costs little; no stop costs carless residents their shifts. If we act, what follows? If we don't?

Notice what happened: five minutes of methodical work produced more material than an hour of waiting for inspiration. Invention is a search procedure, not a mood.

Stasis theory: find the real question

Stasis theory — the master tool. Before arguing, establish what kind of disagreement this is: conjecture (did it happen?), definition (what do we call it?), quality (was it justified?), policy (what should we do?). Most doomed arguments are people answering different stasis questions past each other.

StasisThe questionExample
ConjectureDid it happen?"Did the driver run the red light?"
DefinitionWhat do we call it?"Was that 'reckless' or just careless?"
QualityWas it justified?"He was rushing to the hospital — excusable?"
PolicyWhat should we do?"Install a camera, fine him, or do nothing?"

A classic doomed exchange: one neighbor says "You never asked before building the fence" (conjecture — did the asking happen?), the other says "The fence is fine, it looks good" (quality — is it acceptable?). Both can be right about their own question and the argument still goes nowhere, because they never agreed on the question. Step one of every argument: name the stasis out loud.

The enthymeme: rhetoric's syllogism

Logic's syllogism states every premise. Rhetoric's enthymeme leaves one premise unstated — because the audience supplies it. "He shouldn't drive home — he's had four beers." The missing premise (people who've had four beers are unsafe drivers) is left out precisely because everyone already holds it. The audience completes the argument, and an argument you help build feels like your own — which is why enthymemes persuade better than fully spelled-out logic.

Aristotle, who invented the term's technical use, put it plainly:

"The enthymeme is a kind of syllogism." — Aristotle, Rhetoric I.2

The context: Aristotle treats rhetoric as the counterpart (antistrophos) of dialectic — the public, practical twin of formal logic. The enthymeme is logic adapted for real audiences: trimmed of what they already know, aimed at what they must decide. One caution: the unstated premise must actually be true and actually be shared. Smuggle in a false premise and you have not an enthymeme but a trick.

Try
  1. Take a disagreement you're currently in (or observing). Identify its stasis. Then run your position through the five topics and write down the two strongest arguments you hadn't thought of.
  2. Take the position "phones should stay out of bedrooms" and find one honest argument from each of the five topics.
  3. Turn your best argument from exercise 2 into an enthymeme. Write the unstated premise down separately — and check that it is true.
Check
Two people argue about a new law. One cites crime statistics, the other says "it's just wrong." What happened?
They're at different stasis points — one argues consequences (policy/quality), the other argues moral definition. Until they agree on the question, no argument can land.
Check
Your neighbor says "the new bus stop will raise my taxes"; you say "carless people need the bus." Which stases are you each arguing?
Your neighbor is at policy/consequences (what will this cost?), you are at definition/quality (what counts as a need?). Different questions — settle the stasis before trading arguments.
Check
Why is an enthymeme often more persuasive than a fully spelled-out syllogism?
Because the audience completes it. Supplying the missing premise makes the listener a participant in the reasoning — and an argument you helped build feels like your own conclusion, not someone else's imposition.

Summary

Invention is a search procedure: the five topics find arguments on demand, stasis theory makes sure you're answering the right question, and the enthymeme packages the result in the form real audiences actually reason with. Never argue before you know the stasis; never trust inspiration when a checklist will do.

3

Arrangement: the classical speech

▾

Objectives

  • Lay out the six parts of a classical oration from memory.
  • Order arguments inside the confirmatio using primacy and recency.
  • Map the six parts onto a modern format — an email, a video, a pitch.

Cicero's six-part structure still underlies every effective persuasive piece — essays, videos, pitches:

  1. Exordium — the opening: win attention and goodwill. Start with the stakes, not throat-clearing.
  2. Narratio — the facts: a brief, neutral account of what happened. No argument yet.
  3. Partitio — the roadmap: "Here is what I'll show, in this order." (Audiences relax when they know the shape.)
  4. Confirmatio — your case: arguments in ascending order of strength.
  5. Refutatio — answer the objections before the audience raises them. This is where credibility is won.
  6. Peroratio — the close: restate, amplify, and tell them what to do or feel now.

The six parts at work

Watch the structure carry a real request — asking the town council to fix a dangerous crosswalk:

  • Exordium: "Last month, two children were nearly hit at the Main Street crosswalk." (Stakes first. No "thank you for having me.")
  • Narratio: The crossing has no signal; 400 cars pass per hour at school dismissal; three near-misses are on record. Facts only — no adjectives, no argument yet.
  • Partitio: "I'll show you the danger, the cheap fix, and why the cost objection doesn't hold." (The audience now knows the shape and can relax into it.)
  • Confirmatio: Three arguments, weakest to strongest: the near-miss records; the traffic counts at dismissal; the cost of one injury claim versus the cost of paint and a sign.
  • Refutatio: "Yes, the budget is tight — but the fix costs less than a single injury claim, and the liability of doing nothing costs more than both."
  • Peroratio: "Two kids, one crosswalk, four hundred dollars. Vote yes tonight."

Cicero, who systematized this structure as a young man in De Inventione, defined the canon itself:

"Dispositio est rerum inventarum in ordinem distributio" — "Arrangement is the distribution of the things invented into their proper order." — Cicero, De Inventione I.9

The context matters: rerum inventarum, "the things invented" — arrangement assumes invention is already done. You cannot arrange what you have not found. The six parts turn a pile of discovered arguments into a campaign with a beginning, a middle, and an end.

Ordering inside the confirmatio

Put arguments in ascending order of strength: second-strongest first, strongest last. Two memory effects make this work. Primacy: the first argument earns the audience's attention and frames everything after it — so open with something genuinely strong, never a warm-up. Recency: the last argument is what the audience carries into the refutatio and the peroratio — so close with your best, and let its momentum do the final pushing. The weak and medium arguments go in the middle, where they are carried along by the frame. Never bury your strongest point mid-list, and never spend it first on an audience that isn't warmed up yet.

The six parts in modern dress

Classical partIn an emailIn a video essay
ExordiumSubject line + first line: the stakesCold open: the hook, first 15 seconds
NarratioBackground paragraph, facts only"Here's what happened" segment
Partitio"Here's what I'm asking, in three parts""In this video: three claims"

PeroratioThe ask + deadlineFinal 30 seconds: the takeaway

Once you see the pattern, you cannot unsee it: the best explainers, pitches, and essays all run this skeleton, whether their authors know Cicero or not. The structure is not a ritual — it is the order in which human minds accept being persuaded.

Try
  1. Outline a 3-minute talk (a request to a boss, a toast, a complaint that needs fixing) using all six parts. Keep the whole outline under 150 words.
  2. Take a persuasive email you once wrote that failed. Label which of the six parts it skipped — that gap is usually the reason it failed.
  3. Watch a favorite video essay and timestamp where each of the six parts begins.
Check
Why put your strongest argument last in the confirmatio rather than first?
Recency and momentum: the audience carries your strongest point into the refutatio and peroratio. Leading with it spends your best ammunition before the audience is warmed up — and weakens the close.
Check
Why does the partitio come before the confirmatio instead of after it?
Audiences reason better when they know the shape in advance. A roadmap lowers cognitive load — attention goes to evaluating the arguments instead of wondering where the talk is headed.
Check
What breaks if you skip the refutatio?
The audience raises the objection themselves, unanswered — and your silence reads as concession. Answering objections before they surface is where credibility is won; skipping it surrenders that ground.

Summary

The six-part oration — exordium, narratio, partitio, confirmatio, refutatio, peroratio — is the order in which minds accept persuasion. Order the case itself for primacy and recency, answer objections before they are raised, and close by telling the audience what to do now. The structure fits any medium because it fits the mind.

4

Style: clarity is the virtue

▾

Objectives

  • Match the style level — plain, middle, grand — to the occasion.
  • Rewrite abstract, passive, throat-clearing sentences in plain style.
  • Use one rhetorical device well, and know when to stop.

The ancients named three levels of style — plain (teach), middle (please), grand (move) — and the master knew when each was due. For almost all modern purposes, the plain style wins. Its rules:

  • Concrete over abstract: "the 2018 van" beats "the vehicle asset."
  • Short sentences for punch, long for flow — vary deliberately.
  • Active voice unless the actor is genuinely unknown or irrelevant.
  • Cut throat-clearing: "It is important to note that" → delete.

The three levels

LevelJobExample
PlainTeach"The bus leaves at 6:10 from Main Street."
MiddlePlease"Catch the early bus and you'll be there with time for coffee."
GrandMove"At dawn, the bus carries you toward the life you're building."

Notice: each level is honest. The grand style is not fancy words — it is high stakes honestly felt. Mismatching the level is the real vice: grand language for routine matters sounds pompous and destroys trust, while plain language at a funeral sounds cold. The master skill is not elevation but fit.

Virtues and vices

Quintilian ranked the virtues of style, and put one first:

"The first essential of oratory is clearness" — perspicuitas. — Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria, Book VIII

The context: Quintilian lists clarity before ornament, correctness, and grandeur. Figures of speech exist to serve clarity, not replace it — a decorated fog is still fog. Measure every sentence against the vices:

ViceExampleFix
Throat-clearing"It is important to note that the meeting starts at noon."Delete the throat: "The meeting starts at noon."
Passive fog"Mistakes were made in the handling of the funds."Name the actor: "I missed the deadline."
Abstract nouns"We must optimize our vehicle assets."Concretize: "We must fix the 2018 van."
Nominalizations"Make a decision about the implementation."Use the verb: "Decide how to implement it."
Vague intensifiers"The delay was very, very significant."Give the number: "The delay was three weeks."

Devices that earn their keep

A few schemes (patterns) that actually earn their keep: tricolon (three parallel phrases — "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness"), anaphora (repetition at the start — "we shall fight… we shall fight…"), antithesis ("not X but Y"). Use sparingly; a speech full of devices is a fireworks show, not an argument. The rule: one device per piece, placed at the emotional peak. A tricolon in the peroratio lands; the same tricolon in the narratio distracts. And every device must carry real content — "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness" works because each term adds meaning, not because three sounds nice.

Try
  1. Rewrite this in plain style: "It is imperative that we leverage our core competencies to operationalize a paradigm shift in customer-facing deliverables." Then rewrite it once more in the grand style — and notice which one you'd actually trust.
  2. Take 100 words of your own writing and cut 30% of the words without losing any meaning. What survived was the content; what died was the fog.
  3. Write one tricolon about something you actually believe. Read it aloud — if it sounds performed rather than felt, cut it.
Check
When is the grand style appropriate?
When the audience must be moved to costly action — eulogies, crisis speeches, calls to sacrifice. Using it for routine matters sounds pompous and destroys trust.
Check
Why is "It is important to note that" a vice rather than just a harmless habit?
It announces importance instead of being important — pure throat-clearing. Delete it and the sentence stands on its own; the phrase was doing no work except delaying the point.
Check
A eulogy is full of tricolons and anaphora but says nothing specific about the person who died. What's wrong?
Devices without content — ornament substituting for invention. Grand style needs true stakes and concrete particulars; figures of speech can't carry a speech that has nothing to say.

Summary

Clarity is the first virtue of style, and fit is the master skill: plain to teach, middle to please, grand to move — matched to real stakes. Cut throat-clearing, passive fog, and abstractions; spend one well-placed device per piece. A clear sentence is persuasive; a decorated fog is not.

5

Ethos, pathos, logos — in that order

▾

Objectives

  • Explain why audiences process ethos first, pathos second, logos last.
  • Identify all three appeals operating in a real persuasive text.
  • Apply the full-disclosure test to tell persuasion from manipulation.

Aristotle's three appeals are usually listed logos-first. In practice, audiences process them in reverse order of prestige:

  • Ethos (character): Should I trust this person? Decided in the first minute — from honesty signals, admitted limits, and visible fairness (including steelmanning opponents).
  • Pathos (emotion): Do I care? Stories, concrete particulars, and stakes move people; abstractions don't.
  • Logos (reason): Does it hold up? The argument itself — which only gets a hearing after ethos and pathos open the door.

Why the order matters

The audience asks should I trust this person? before it asks does this hold up? — and no argument survives a failed trust check. A hostile audience filters your logos out before evaluating it: the premises are suspected, the evidence is discounted, the conclusion is resisted. Ethos is the gate. Then pathos opens attention: people act on what they feel, and a story about one real person outperforms a statistic about ten thousand — not because audiences are irrational, but because attention is scarce and emotion is what allocates it. Only then does logos get its hearing, as the payload the first two carried in.

Aristotle's own definition of the art explains why all three count as means of persuasion:

"Rhetoric may be defined as the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion." — Aristotle, Rhetoric I.2

Note the word available: rhetoric works with the means the situation actually offers — including the audience's character, the speaker's reputation, and the emotions legitimately at stake. Character and feeling are not corruptions of argument; they are part of the material the art works with.

Reading a speech through the three lenses

Here is a short appeal to fix the dangerous crosswalk, with each move labeled. Watch how the three appeals cooperate:

[Ethos] "I drive this road twice a day, and I've reported this crossing three times. I'm not here to blame the town — the budget is real, and I know it." [Pathos] "Last month a stroller had to back into traffic because the curb ramp was blocked." [Logos] "The fix costs four hundred dollars. One injury claim costs fifty thousand. The math is not close."

The ethos moves: firsthand knowledge, admitted limits, fairness to the other side ("the budget is real"). The pathos move: one concrete particular — a stroller, backing into traffic — instead of "safety concerns." The logos: two numbers and a comparison. Remove any one appeal and the speech weakens: without ethos the numbers are suspect, without pathos nobody listens, without logos it is just a sad story.

The ethical line

The ethical line: pathos in service of a true logos is persuasion. Pathos as a substitute for logos is manipulation. The test is whether the emotional appeal would survive the audience learning the full facts. Two practical tests:

  • Full disclosure: if the audience learned everything you know, would the appeal still work? If yes, it is persuasion.
  • Substitution: remove the emotion — does an argument remain? If nothing remains, the feeling was the whole case, and that is manipulation.

Aristotle put character first among the three proofs, and said why:

"The character of the speaker is a cause of persuasion when the speech is so uttered as to make him worthy of belief." — Aristotle, Rhetoric 1356a

The context: of the three pisteis (proofs), ethos comes first in Aristotle's own order — before emotion, before argument. Trustworthiness is not decoration on the argument; it is the first argument.

Try
  1. Recall a speech, ad, or video that moved you. Identify one ethos move, one pathos move, and the actual logos underneath. Was the logos solid, or was it decoration?
  2. Write a 100-word pitch for something you want. Label your own ethos, pathos, and logos moves — and cut any pathos move that fails the substitution test.
  3. Find an ad that repels you. Name the exact move that broke trust — usually a faked ethos signal or pathos with no logos behind it.
Check
What's the fastest way to build ethos with a skeptical audience?
Concede something real: admit a limit, grant the opponent's strongest point, or disclose an interest. Costly honesty is the most credible signal there is.
Check
Why can't logos go first with a hostile audience?
A hostile audience never grants the hearing logos needs. Without trust (ethos) the premises are suspected; without attention (pathos) the argument is never processed. The gate and the door come before the payload.
Check
A charity ad shows a suffering child but never says where donations go. Persuasion or manipulation?
Manipulation. The pathos substitutes for logos — there is no argument underneath, only feeling. It fails both tests: full disclosure would change the response, and removing the emotion leaves nothing.

Summary

Audiences process ethos, then pathos, then logos — trust opens the gate, feeling opens attention, reason delivers the payload. Each appeal has a job the others cannot do, and the ethical line is whether the emotion serves a true argument or substitutes for one. Build ethos with costly honesty; spend pathos on true particulars.

6

Memory and delivery: the method of loci

▾

Objectives

  • Memorize an ordered 10-item list using the method of loci.
  • Tell the Simonides story and state what it proves about memory.
  • Apply the five delivery basics the next time you speak.

Roman orators spoke for hours without notes using the method of loci: associate each point with a location along a familiar route (your house, your walk to work), in order. To recall, mentally walk the route. It works because human memory is spatial first, verbal second — modern memory champions still use exactly this.

Simonides and the banquet

The technique's origin story comes from Cicero. Simonides of Ceos, a lyric poet, was dining at a banquet when he was called outside; moments later the roof collapsed, crushing the guests beyond recognition. When relatives asked which body was which, Simonides identified every guest — by remembering where each had been sitting. The seating order had burned the names into his spatial memory, and the disaster could not erase the map.

"Persons desiring to train this faculty must select places and form mental images of the things they wish to remember." — Cicero, De Oratore, on Simonides' discovery

The context: Cicero credits Simonides as the inventor of the art of memory, and draws the principle — it is order that gives memory its strength. Places hold the order; images hold the content. Two thousand years later, every memory champion on earth uses the same two ingredients: a fixed route and vivid pictures.

Building your own palace

StepDo thisExample: a 10-item grocery list
1. RoutePick a familiar path with distinct stations, in fixed orderYour home: doormat, couch, kitchen table, stove, fridge, sink, hallway, bedroom door, bed, closet
2. ImagesMake each item vivid, absurd, and active — the stranger the betterMilk: a gallon flooding the doormat. Eggs: hatching on the couch cushions
3. PlaceSet one image at each station, in list orderBread: a loaf the size of the kitchen table. Apples: rolling off the stove. Cheese: melting down the fridge door. Coffee: pouring from the faucet. Rice: a sack burst in the hallway. Beans: rattling cans at the bedroom door. Soap: a foam party on the bed. Batteries: sparking in the closet
4. WalkTo recall, mentally walk the route and read off the stationsDoormat → milk; couch → eggs; kitchen table → bread… forward, backward, or from any station

Why the images must be strange: distinctive pictures resist interference. A normal egg on a normal table blends with ten thousand ordinary memories; a hatching egg on your couch is unique and therefore retrievable. Absurdity is a feature, not a joke. And why the route: the stations give you ordered recall — you can start at station six, go backward, or jump in anywhere, which rote chains cannot do.

Delivery basics (actio): speak slightly slower than feels natural; pause after important sentences (silence is punctuation); plant your feet; gesture from the shoulder, not the wrist; look at one person at a time. Nerves are fuel — the goal isn't calm, it's controlled energy. Three additions: project from the diaphragm, not the throat — volume should feel easy, not loud; let sentences fall at the end rather than rising (uptalk turns statements into questions); and hold the final pause after your last line — never apologize for finishing.

Try
  1. Memorize a 10-item grocery list with the method of loci: place each item somewhere along your walk through your home. Test yourself an hour later, then tomorrow.
  2. Place the six parts of your capstone speech along your home route — one part per station — and walk it twice before you rehearse aloud.
  3. Record 60 seconds of yourself delivering an exordium. Watch for wrist gestures, uptalk, and rushed pauses — then record it once more, slower.
Check
Why does the method of loci work better than rote repetition?
It piggybacks on the brain's ancient spatial-navigation system, which is far stronger than its verbal rehearsal loop — and it adds vivid, distinctive, ordered retrieval cues instead of one fragile chain.
Check
Why must the memory images be vivid and strange rather than plain and realistic?
Distinctive images resist interference. A plain egg on a plain table blends with thousands of ordinary memories; a hatching egg on your couch is unique — and what is unique is retrievable.
Check
You're shaking before you speak. What's the correct response?
Don't fight the nerves — aim them. Adrenaline is fuel; controlled energy reads as conviction. The goal isn't calm, it's directed intensity: slower pace, planted feet, deliberate pauses.

Summary

Memory is spatial: fixed places hold the order, vivid images hold the content — the method Simonides discovered at a collapsed banquet still wins memory championships. Delivery is the physical channel of the argument: slower than feels natural, silence as punctuation, nerves aimed rather than suppressed. Own the speech, then land it.

★

Capstone: the three-minute speech

▾

Objectives

  • Write a complete six-part persuasive speech of about 400 words.
  • Deliver it from memory — no notes — to a real listener or a camera.
  • Judge it by what survives in the listener's memory after 24 hours.

Write and deliver (aloud, to a person or a camera) a three-minute persuasive speech:

  1. Use the six-part arrangement.
  2. Include a refutatio — answer your strongest objection honestly.
  3. One tricolon or anaphora, no more.
  4. Memorize it with the method of loci — no notes.

The assignment, unpacked

Three minutes at a natural speaking pace is roughly 380–420 words — write it out fully, then time yourself reading aloud and cut or expand to land between 2:45 and 3:15. Pick a position you actually hold; a borrowed conviction cannot survive the refutatio. For the refutatio, steelman the single strongest objection — the one that worries you — and answer it fairly; a strawman here fails the whole exercise. Place your one device (a tricolon or an anaphora) at the emotional peak, usually in the peroratio — one device, no more. For memory, assign the six parts to six stations on your home route and put the key sentence of each part at its station; you are memorizing the structure and the turns, not every word.

How to rehearse

Write the speech. Time it aloud — silent rehearsal lies about pace, so always rehearse on your feet and out loud. Walk your memory palace twice, then deliver without the text. Record yourself on your phone and watch for the delivery basics: pace, pauses, feet, shoulder gestures, one person at a time. Fix one thing per run, not five. Deliver to a person if you can; a camera if you can't. The camera is honest and the person is harder — do both if possible.

The rubric

CriterionPass looks likeFail looks like
StructureAll six parts present, in order, partitio honestParts missing, merged, or mislabeled
RefutatioStrongest objection answered fairlyStrawman, or the objection dodged
DeliveryPaced, paused, no notes, lands the endingRushed, reading, trailing off at the close
RecallListener remembers the ask tomorrowListener remembers only the performance

After the delivery

Persuasion is measured in the listener, not in you — so collect the data. The next day, ask what they remember and write down their exact words before you judge your own performance. Then diagnose by canon: if they remember the story but not the ask, your peroratio failed; if they remember the ask but not the reasons, your confirmatio was thin; if they remember the performance but nothing else, invention never happened. Revise exactly one canon and deliver again. That loop — deliver, measure recall, fix one canon — is the whole art in miniature, and it is also how the ancients trained: not by theorizing about speeches, but by giving them and learning what survived.

Then evaluate: did you persuade, or merely perform? Ask your listener what they remember tomorrow. What survives 24 hours is what worked.

Try
  1. Deliver the speech twice — once to a person, once to the camera. Note what changes between the two: pace, energy, which parts wobble.
  2. The next day, ask your listener: "What do you remember, and what would you do?" Write down their exact words before you judge your own performance.
Check
Why "no notes" for the capstone?
Notes keep the speech outside you; the method of loci forces internalization. A speech you own can bend to the room — skip, expand, answer a question — while a script shatters the moment you lose your place.
Check
What's the real test of the speech — applause, or something else?
Something else: what survives 24 hours in the listener's memory, and whether they would act. Applause measures the performance; recall measures the persuasion.

Summary

The capstone fuses all six lessons: invention finds the case, arrangement orders it, style words it, memory owns it, delivery lands it — and ethos, pathos, and logos run through the whole. Write it, palace it, deliver it, and let a listener's 24-hour memory be the judge.
