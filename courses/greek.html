Tongues II

Greek I

Learn to read Ancient Greek — the language of Homer, Plato, and the New Testament. Six lessons from the alphabet to your first real texts.

1

The alphabet: twenty-four letters

▾

Greek writes left to right, like English, and many capitals will look familiar — the Roman alphabet was borrowed from Greek. There are 24 letters. Learn their names; classicists refer to letters by name the way musicians refer to notes.

We use the Erasmian pronunciation — the reconstructed classroom system used in schools, which distinguishes letters modern Greek has merged. It is not how Greeks speak today, but it keeps ancient wordplay and poetry audible.

Try
Transliterate these English words back into Greek letters: philosophia, demokratia, biblia. Sound each one out as you write it.
Check
How many letters are in the Greek alphabet, and what is special about sigma?
24 letters. Sigma has two lowercase forms: σ inside a word, ς at the end (λόγος). Same letter, same sound — the shape just marks the word's edge.
2

Breathings, accents, and diphthongs

▾

Three small marks turn letter-sounds into real words.

Breathings. Every word beginning with a vowel (or rho) carries a breathing mark. The rough breathing (῾) adds an h: ἁρμονία is "harmonia." The smooth breathing (ι) adds nothing: Ἀθήνη is just "Athene." On capitals the mark stands before the letter: Ἑλλάς ("Hellas"). Initial rho always takes the rough breathing: ῥήτωρ ("rhetor").

Accents. Ancient Greek was a pitch-accent language; in Erasmian reading we simply stress the accented syllable. Three marks: acute (´), grave (`), circumflex (῀). Three rules cover nearly everything:

  • The acute can stand on any of the last three syllables.
  • The circumflex can stand on one of the last two, and only over a long vowel or diphthong.
  • The grave appears only on the last syllable, replacing an acute when another word follows.

Diphthongs. Two vowels gliding as one syllable:

DiphthongSounds like
αιai in aisle
ειei in eight
οιoi in oil
αυow in cow
ευeu in feud
ουoo in boot
υιrounded "ee" gliding to "ee" (rare)

You will also meet ᾳ ῃ ῳ — a long vowel with a tiny iota written beneath (iota subscript). In Erasmian reading the iota is silent; just pronounce the long vowel.

Sound these out, marking breathings and diphthongs before you speak: ἄνθρωπος (AN-thro-pos, "human"), λόγος (LO-gos, "word"), φιλία (phi-LEE-a, "friendship"), Ἑλλάς (hel-LAS, "Greece"). If you can sound out words, you can read anything — slowly, but truly.

Try
Sound out these words aloud, marking the breathing and any diphthong first: αὐτός, εἰρήνη, οἶκος, θεός. Then check: ow-TOS ("self"), ay-RAY-nay ("peace"), OY-kos ("house"), theh-OS ("god").
Check
In Ἑλλάς, what does the mark over the ε tell you to do?
It is a rough breathing — pronounce an h: "hel-LAS," not "el-las." The mark stands before the capital epsilon because breathings precede capitals.
3

Nouns: the second declension

▾

Like Latin (see Grammar, lesson 4), Greek marks a noun's job with its ending. Three cases do most of the work: the nominative (subject — who acts), the accusative (object — who is acted upon), and the genitive (possession or "of"). The second declension covers masculine nouns ending in -ος and neuter nouns ending in -ον.

Masculine: λόγος (word, reason)

SingularPlural
Nominativeλόγοςλόγοι
Genitiveλόγουλόγων
Dativeλόγῳλόγοις
Accusativeλόγονλόγους
Vocativeλόγελόγοι

Neuter: δῶρον (gift)

SingularPlural
Nominativeδῶρονδῶρα
Genitiveδώρουδώρων
Dativeδώρῳδώροις
Accusativeδῶρονδῶρα
Vocativeδῶρονδῶρα

The great neuter rule: nominative = accusative, always, in every number. (The dative means "to/for," the vocative is direct address — you will use them less at first.)

The article ("the") declines too, and it is your best friend — it announces a noun's case before you finish reading the ending:

  • ὁ λόγος — the word (subject) · τὸν λόγον — the word (object) · τοῦ λόγου — of the word
  • τὸ δῶρον — the gift (subject or object) · τοῦ δώρου — of the gift · τὰ δῶρα — the gifts

Read: ὁ φίλος βλέπει τὸ δῶρον. — "The friend sees the gift." τὸ δῶρον τοῦ φίλου. — "The gift of the friend."

Try
Decline φίλος (friend) in the nominative, accusative, and genitive — singular and plural. Cover the tables above first.
Check
Parse λόγον: case, number, and its job in a sentence.
Accusative singular. It is the direct object — "the word" as the thing acted upon: e.g. γράφω τὸν λόγον, "I write the word."
4

Verbs: the present tense and εἰμί

▾

Greek verbs are machines: a stem (the meaning) plus an ending (who does it). Most verbs follow the -ω conjugation. Take λυ- (loose, free) + endings:

SingularPlural
1st (I / we)λύωλύομεν
2nd (you)λύειςλύετε
3rd (he/she/it / they)λύειλύουσι(ν)

The present tense covers "I loose," "I am loosing," and "I do loose" — English splits what Greek leaves open. The movable nu (-ν) is often added to 3rd-person forms, especially before a vowel or at a sentence's end: λέγουσιν.

Then memorize the one verb that refuses the pattern — εἰμί (to be):

SingularPlural
1stεἰμί (I am)ἐσμέν (we are)
2ndεἶ (you are)ἐστέ (you are)
3rdἐστί(ν) (he/she/it is)εἰσί(ν) (they are)

Now read whole sentences: ὁ ἄνθρωπος γράφει. — "The man is writing." οἱ φίλοι λέγουσιν. — "The friends speak." ἔχω βιβλίον. — "I have a book." ὁ θεὸς ἐστιν ἀγαθός. — "God is good" (ἀγαθός = good).

Try
Conjugate γράφω (I write) in the present active indicative — all six forms. Then translate βλέπομεν.
Check
Parse λύετε: person, number, meaning.
2nd person plural — "you (all) loose / are loosing." The -ετε ending always marks "you plural" in the -ω conjugation.

Greek alive in English. Grammar (lesson 2) gave you the root table — phon, graph, log, phil, soph, dem, chron. Here is the next layer: whole Greek words walking around in English sentences.

  • σχολή (leisure) → school — the Greeks thought learning was what free people did with free time.
  • γυμνάσιον → gymnasium — the place of exercise (from γυμνός, naked).
  • θέατρον (viewing place) → theater · δρᾶμα (a thing done) → drama · μουσική (art of the Muses) → music.
  • χάος → chaos · κῦδος (glory) → kudos.
  • οἰκονομία (household management: οἶκος + νόμος) → economy · πολιτικά (affairs of the πόλις) → politics · αὐτοκρατία (self-rule) → autocracy.
  • τῆλε (far) + φωνή (sound, voice) → telephone · μικρός + σκοπέω (I look) → microscope · πᾶς + δῆμος (people) → pandemic.
  • φόβος (fear) → -phobia · φῶς (light) → photo- · ὕδωρ (water) → hydro- · θερμός (hot) → thermometer · ἄστρον (star) → astronomy · γῆ (earth) → geography.
Try
Decode these English words from their Greek parts: telephone, microscope, autocracy, pandemic. Name the Greek word behind each half.
Check
What two Greek words make "philosophy," and what do they literally mean?
φίλος (loved one, friend — "love") + σοφία (wisdom) = φιλοσοφία, "love of wisdom." Not "wisdom itself" — the love of it. The name is a confession of incompleteness.
★

Capstone: your first Greek reading

▾

Two real texts. Read each aloud first (lesson 2's rules), then work through the gloss with the grammar you now own.

1. Xenophon, Anabasis, opening (slightly simplified). In 401 BC Xenophon marched with ten thousand Greek mercenaries into Persia. This is his first sentence:

Δαρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος γίγνονται παῖδες δύο· πρεσβύτερος μὲν Ἀρταξέρξης, νεώτερος δὲ Κῦρος.
WordFormMeaning
Δαρείουgenitive of Δαρεῖοςof Darius
καὶ—and
Παρυσάτιδοςgenitive of Παρυσᾶτιςof Parysatis
γίγνονται3rd plural present of γίγνομαιare born
παῖδεςnominative plural of παῖςsons, children
δύο—two
πρεσβύτεροςnominative masculineelder
μὲν … δὲ—on the one hand … on the other
ἈρταξέρξηςnominativeArtaxerxes
νεώτεροςnominative masculineyounger

Translation: "To Darius and Parysatis are born two sons: the elder Artaxerxes, the younger Cyrus." (γίγνομαι and the μὲν…δὲ construction are new — the gloss carries them; everything else is lessons 1–5.)

2. The opening line of John's Gospel — the most-read Greek sentence in history:

Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.
  • Ἐν — in (+ dative)
  • ἀρχῇ — dative of ἀρχή, "beginning" → in the beginning
  • ἦν — "was" (a past tense of εἰμί; Greek II teaches this form)
  • ὁ λόγος — the Word
  • πρὸς τὸν θεόν — with God (πρός + accusative, "toward")
  • θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος — θεός has no article because it is the predicate: "the Word was God." The article marks the subject; its absence marks what is said about it.
Try
Read both passages aloud using lesson 2's rules — breathings, accents, diphthongs. Then cover the glosses and translate the Xenophon sentence yourself before checking.
Check
Translate the opening line of John's Gospel into English.
"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." Word by word: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ("in the beginning") ἦν ("was") ὁ λόγος ("the Word"), καὶ ("and") ὁ λόγος ἦν ("the Word was") πρὸς τὸν θεόν ("with God"), καὶ ("and") θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος ("the Word was God" — θεός without the article is the predicate).
