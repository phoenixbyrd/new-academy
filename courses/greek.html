Tongues II

Greek I

Learn to read Ancient Greek — the language of Homer, Plato, and the New Testament. Six lessons from the alphabet to your first real texts.

1

The alphabet: twenty-four letters

▾

Objectives

  • Name all 24 letters in order, alpha to omega.
  • Recognize capitals and lowercase forms, including sigma's two shapes.
  • Transliterate Greek words into English letters and read them aloud.

Greek writes left to right, like English, and many capitals will look familiar — the Roman alphabet was borrowed from Greek. There are 24 letters. Learn their names; classicists refer to letters by name the way musicians refer to notes.

We use the Erasmian pronunciation — the reconstructed classroom system used in schools, which distinguishes letters modern Greek has merged. It is not how Greeks speak today, but it keeps ancient wordplay and poetry audible.

Capitals you already know

Fourteen of the 24 capitals are identical to Roman letters: Α Β Ε Η Ι Κ Μ Ν Ο Ρ Τ Χ Ζ — alpha, beta, epsilon, eta, iota, kappa, mu, nu, omicron, rho, tau, chi, zeta. But looks deceive. Η (eta) is a long a sound, not an h. Ρ (rho) is an r, not a p. Χ (chi) is the guttural in Bach, not an x. Ν (nu) is an n — the v-shape is a coincidence. Learn the names and these four traps stop being traps.

The genuinely new shapes are the ones that never entered the Roman alphabet: Γ Δ Θ Λ Ξ Π Σ Φ Ψ Ω — gamma, delta, theta, lambda, xi, pi, sigma, phi, psi, omega. Say each name aloud as you write the letter; the name is the handle your memory will grab later when you meet the letter inside a word.

Reading Greek into English letters

Transliteration is your training wheels: write the English equivalent under each Greek letter and the word unlocks. φιλοσοφία becomes philosophia; δημοκρατία becomes demokratia; βιβλία becomes biblia. Notice how much English is hiding in Greek spelling — transliteration makes the borrowing visible.

Your first real text is a famous one, and you can already sound it out. These are Socrates' words as Plato reports them, spoken at Socrates' trial in Athens in 399 BC (Apology 38a):

ὁ δὲ ἀνεξέταστος βίος οὐ βιωτὸς ἀνθρώπῳ.
ho de anexetastos bios ou biōtos anthrōpōi.
"The unexamined life is not worth living for a human being."

You don't know the grammar yet — lesson 2's marks and lesson 5's vocabulary will open it further — but the point stands: with the alphabet alone, you can pronounce a sentence that has been quoted for 2,400 years.

Try
  1. Transliterate these English words back into Greek letters: philosophia, demokratia, biblia. Sound each one out as you write it.
  2. Sound out these Greek words and say the English word hiding inside each: φῶς ("light" — photo-), λόγος ("word" — -logy), φιλία ("friendship" — philo-).
  3. Write the alphabet from memory, alpha to omega, then check yourself against the table. Circle the letters you missed and drill just those.
Check
How many letters are in the Greek alphabet, and what is special about sigma?
24 letters. Sigma has two lowercase forms: σ inside a word, ς at the end (λόγος). Same letter, same sound — the shape just marks the word's edge.
Check
Transliterate Ὅμηρος. Whose name is it?
Homer — Ὅμηρος is the Greek name of Homer, author of the Iliad and Odyssey. Letter by letter: Ὁ=H (rough breathing on the vowel), μ=m, η=ē, ρ=r, ο=o, ς=s.
Check
What do "alpha" and "omega" mean as a pair — as in "I am the alpha and the omega"?
Alpha is the first letter and omega the last, so "alpha and omega" means "the beginning and the end" — the whole of everything. (The phrase is from Revelation 1:8.) It only makes sense once you know the alphabet's order.

Summary

Twenty-four letters, alpha to omega, with Erasmian sounds. Fourteen capitals match Roman letters but four are traps (Η, Ρ, Χ, Ν); sigma changes shape at word end. Transliteration turns Greek spelling into readable English letters — and English is full of Greek hiding in plain sight.

2

Breathings, accents, and diphthongs

▾

Objectives

  • Read rough and smooth breathings, including on capitals and initial rho.
  • Place and pronounce the three accents under the placement rules, including the σωτῆρα rule.
  • Recognize all seven diphthongs and the iota subscript.

Three small marks turn letter-sounds into real words.

Breathing before you speak

Breathings. Every word beginning with a vowel (or rho) carries a breathing mark. The rough breathing (῾) adds an h: ἁρμονία is "harmonia." The smooth breathing (ι) adds nothing: Ἀθήνη is just "Athene." On capitals the mark stands before the letter: Ἑλλάς ("Hellas"). Initial rho always takes the rough breathing: ῥήτωρ ("rhetor").

Two details worth knowing now. First, a word beginning with the diphthong ου or any diphthong takes its breathing on the second vowel of the diphthong: οὗτος ("this") is pronounced "HOU-tos" — the rough breathing sits over the υ but applies to the whole syllable. Second, the diaeresis (¨) marks two vowels that are not a diphthong: in Μωϋσῆς (Mōysēs, "Moses") the two dots tell you to pronounce υ and η as separate syllables. When you see ¨, split; when you don't, glide.

The three accents

Accents. Ancient Greek was a pitch-accent language; in Erasmian reading we simply stress the accented syllable. Three marks: acute (´), grave (`), circumflex (῀). Three rules cover nearly everything:

  • The acute can stand on any of the last three syllables.
  • The circumflex can stand on one of the last two, and only over a long vowel or diphthong.
  • The grave appears only on the last syllable, replacing an acute when another word follows.

Now the refinement that makes the circumflex predictable — the σωτῆρα rule. A circumflex may stand on the penult (second-to-last syllable) only if the ultima (last syllable) is short. The mnemonic is σωτῆρα ("savior," accusative): the penult τῆ carries the circumflex because the final α is short. If the ultima were long, the accent would be forced forward into an acute. Watch it happen in lesson 3: δῶρον (circumflex on the penult, short ultima -ον) becomes δώρου in the genitive (ultima now long -ου, so the accent becomes an acute and shifts to the penult). Nouns keep their accent on the same syllable through the cases — the accent only changes shape when the rules require it.

Verbs play by a different convention: the recessive accent. A verb's accent retreats as far toward the front of the word as the three-syllable limit allows (λύομεν, not *λυόμεν — you'll see why in lesson 4). So when you see an accent sitting as far left as it can, you're probably looking at a verb; when the accent sits on a fixed syllable through every form, you're probably looking at a noun.

Diphthongs and the iota beneath

Diphthongs. Two vowels gliding as one syllable:

Sound these out, marking breathings and diphthongs before you speak: ἄνθρωπος (AN-thro-pos, "human"), λόγος (LO-gos, "word"), φιλία (phi-LEE-a, "friendship"), Ἑλλάς (hel-LAS, "Greece"). If you can sound out words, you can read anything — slowly, but truly.

A first prayer

Put the marks to work on a famous line — the opening of the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9):

Πάτερ ἡμῶν ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς.
Pater hēmōn ho en tois ouranois.
"Our Father, who is in the heavens."

Spot the machinery: ἡμῶν has a smooth breathing (no h — "hay-MOHN" would be wrong); οὐρανοῖς contains two diphthongs (ου and οι) and a circumflex over the long ῖ; τοῖς shows the iota subscript under ω. The whole sentence is breathings, diphthongs, and accents doing exactly what this lesson taught.

Try
  1. Sound out these words aloud, marking the breathing and any diphthong first: αὐτός, εἰρήνη, οἶκος, θεός. Then check: ow-TOS ("self"), ay-RAY-nay ("peace"), OY-kos ("house"), theh-OS ("god").
  2. Mark the breathings and diphthongs in the Lord's Prayer line above before reading it aloud. Which word has a rough breathing? Which has two diphthongs?
  3. Explain the accent change from δῶρον to δώρου using the σωτῆρα rule.
Check
In Ἑλλάς, what does the mark over the ε tell you to do?
It is a rough breathing — pronounce an h: "hel-LAS," not "el-las." The mark stands before the capital epsilon because breathings precede capitals.
Check
State the σωτῆρα rule in one sentence.
A circumflex can stand on the penult only if the ultima is short — if the ultima is long, the accent becomes an acute instead. Named for σωτῆρα, where the circumflex sits on τῆ because the final α is short.
Check
ἁρμονία and Ἀθήνη both start with a vowel. Why does one get an h and the other not?
ἁρμονία carries the rough breathing (῾), which adds /h/: "harmonia." Ἀθήνη carries the smooth breathing (ι), which adds nothing: "Athene." The mark above (or before, on a capital) the initial vowel tells you which.

Summary

Rough breathing adds h, smooth adds nothing; breathings stand before capitals and always rough on initial rho. The acute ranges over the last three syllables, the circumflex over the last two (long vowels only, and on the penult only when the ultima is short — the σωτῆρα rule), the grave replaces a final acute before another word. Seven diphthongs glide as one syllable; the iota subscript is silent.

3

Nouns: the second declension

▾

Objectives

  • Name the five cases and the job each one does in a sentence.
  • Decline masculine -ος nouns (λόγος) and neuter -ον nouns (δῶρον).
  • Use the article to identify a noun's case on sight.

Like Latin (see Grammar, lesson 4), Greek marks a noun's job with its ending. Three cases do most of the work: the nominative (subject — who acts), the accusative (object — who is acted upon), and the genitive (possession or "of"). The second declension covers masculine nouns ending in -ος and neuter nouns ending in -ον.

Cases are jobs

English still has a trace of cases: he (subject) vs. him (object) vs. his (possession). Greek does this for every noun, with five cases:

  • Nominative — the subject: who acts. ὁ λόγος, "the word" (as subject).
  • Genitive — possession or "of": τοῦ λόγου, "of the word."
  • Dative — "to" or "for": τῷ λόγῳ, "to/for the word." It also means "by/with" in some constructions.
  • Accusative — the direct object: τὸν λόγον, "the word" (as object).
  • Vocative — direct address: ὦ λόγε, "O word!" (the ὦ is optional and often omitted).

Because endings carry the grammar, Greek word order is flexible — the subject can come after the verb and the sentence still parses. That is why learning the endings matters more than learning word order.

Watch the accent do exactly what lesson 2's σωτῆρα rule predicted: δῶρον has a circumflex on the penult because the ultima -ον is short; in δώρου the ultima -ου is long, so the accent becomes an acute. The rules from lesson 2 aren't decoration — they're operating the machinery.

The article is your compass

The article ("the") declines too, and it is your best friend — it announces a noun's case before you finish reading the ending:

  • ὁ λόγος — the word (subject) · τὸν λόγον — the word (object) · τοῦ λόγου — of the word
  • τὸ δῶρον — the gift (subject or object) · τοῦ δώρου — of the gift · τὰ δῶρα — the gifts

Learn the full paradigm — it pays for itself on every page you will ever read:

A verse of pure grammar

The First Epistle of John (4:8) contains a sentence built almost entirely from this lesson's machinery:

ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν.
ho theos agapē estin.
"God is love."

ὁ θεός is nominative — the subject, announced by ὁ. ἀγάπη ("love") is a predicate nominative: it renames the subject, so it stands in the nominative too. ἐστίν is "is" — the verb you will memorize in lesson 4. Two nominatives and a verb: the simplest possible Greek sentence, and one of the most quoted.

Try
  1. Decline φίλος (friend) in the nominative, accusative, and genitive — singular and plural. Cover the tables above first.
  2. In τὸ δῶρον τοῦ φίλου ("the gift of the friend"), parse τοῦ φίλου — case, number, and why.
  3. Translate: οἱ λόγοι τοῦ θεοῦ ("the words of God"). Identify the case of each noun.
Check
Parse λόγον: case, number, and its job in a sentence.
Accusative singular. It is the direct object — "the word" as the thing acted upon: e.g. γράφω τὸν λόγον, "I write the word."
Check
Why is the article especially valuable with neuter nouns?
Because of the neuter rule (nominative = accusative), τὸ δῶρον can be subject or object — the article looks identical either way. You must use word order and context to decide the job, so the article's presence at least tells you a noun is coming and what gender and number it is.
Check
Give the genitive singular and accusative plural of φίλος.
Genitive singular: φίλου ("of the friend"). Accusative plural: φίλους ("the friends," as object). Both follow the λόγος pattern exactly — stem φιλ- plus the -ος endings.

Summary

Five cases mark a noun's job: nominative (subject), genitive (of), dative (to/for), accusative (object), vocative (address). Masculine -ος and neuter -ον nouns follow the λόγος and δῶρον patterns; neuters always match nominative to accusative; accents persist on the same syllable except where the σωτῆρα rule or the genitive-plural rule intervenes. The declining article announces case, gender, and number before you finish the noun.

4

Verbs: the present tense and εἰμί

▾

Objectives

  • Conjugate any -ω verb in the present active indicative.
  • Read and use the movable nu.
  • Memorize εἰμί (to be) in all six forms.

Greek verbs are machines: a stem (the meaning) plus an ending (who does it). Most verbs follow the -ω conjugation. Take λυ- (loose, free) + endings:

SingularPlural
1st (I / we)λύωλύομεν
2nd (you)λύειςλύετε
3rd (he/she/it / they)λύειλύουσι(ν)

Stem plus ending

The endings are the whole system: -ω, -εις, -ει, -ομεν, -ετε, -ουσι(ν). Peel the ending off and the stem gives the meaning; swap the ending and you change the actor. γράφ- (write) gives γράφω, γράφεις, γράφει, γράφομεν, γράφετε, γράφουσι(ν). Because the ending says who acts, Greek routinely drops the subject pronoun — γράφω alone means "I write."

The accent here is recessive (lesson 2): it retreats as far toward the front as the rules allow — λύομεν and λύετε carry the acute on the antepenult because that is as far back as it can go. When you see an accent shoved to the front of a word, suspect a verb; when it sits fixed mid-word through every form, suspect a noun.

The present tense covers "I loose," "I am loosing," and "I do loose" — English splits what Greek leaves open. Context decides which English rendering fits; the Greek simply says the action is happening now.

The movable nu

The movable nu (-ν) is often added to 3rd-person forms, especially before a vowel or at a sentence's end: λέγουσιν.

It is a euphony device — Greek dislikes a vowel crashing into the next word's vowel, and the little -ν cushions the joint. You will see both λέγουσι and λέγουσιν in real texts; they are the same form. Never let a trailing -ν confuse your parsing: strip it mentally and read the ending underneath.

A related sound habit is elision: when a word ending in a short vowel meets a word beginning with a vowel, the first vowel drops and an apostrophe marks the gap — ἀλλά becomes ἀλλ' before a vowel. It is just speech written down.

εἰμί: the verb that refuses the pattern

Then memorize the one verb that refuses the pattern — εἰμί (to be):

SingularPlural
1stεἰμί (I am)ἐσμέν (we are)
2ndεἶ (you are)ἐστέ (you are)
3rdἐστί(ν) (he/she/it is)εἰσί(ν) (they are)

Notice the stem itself changes (εἰ- vs. ἐσ-): that is what makes εἰμί irregular, and why it must be memorized whole rather than built. It is also the most common verb in the language, so the investment pays immediately — including in this course's readings, where ἐστίν has already appeared (ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν, lesson 3).

Reading whole sentences

Now read whole sentences: ὁ ἄνθρωπος γράφει. — "The man is writing." οἱ φίλοι λέγουσιν. — "The friends speak." ἔχω βιβλίον. — "I have a book." ὁ θεὸς ἐστιν ἀγαθός. — "God is good" (ἀγαθός = good).

A few more with the same machinery: ἐγὼ γράφω τὸν λόγον. — "I write the word." οἱ παῖδες βλέπουσι τὰ δῶρα. — "The children see the gifts." ἐσμὲν φίλοι. — "We are friends" (predicate nominative again — friends renames we, so nominative). Each sentence is just lesson 3's nouns meeting lesson 4's verbs.

"I am the light of the world"

The Gospel of John (8:12) gives εἰμί its most famous workout — Jesus speaking:

ἐγώ εἰμι τὸ φῶς τοῦ κόσμου.
egō eimi to phōs tou kosmou.
"I am the light of the world."

Parse it: ἐγώ ("I," lesson 5) is an emphatic subject pronoun — the verb εἰμι already says "I am," so adding ἐγώ stresses I. τὸ φῶς ("the light") is the predicate nominative. τοῦ κόσμου ("of the world") is a genitive of possession hanging off φῶς. φῶς is a third-declension noun you haven't formally met — the gloss carries it here, the way lesson 6's gloss will carry its unknowns.

Try
  1. Conjugate γράφω (I write) in the present active indicative — all six forms. Then translate βλέπομεν.
  2. Conjugate βλέπω in the 1st and 3rd person singular, then use each in a short sentence with τὸ δῶρον.
  3. Translate: ἐσμὲν φίλοι. Then make it negative with οὐ (lesson 5).
Check
Parse λύετε: person, number, meaning.
2nd person plural — "you (all) loose / are loosing." The -ετε ending always marks "you plural" in the -ω conjugation.
Check
Give the 3rd person plural of εἰμί and use it in a three-word sentence.
εἰσί(ν) — "they are." For example: οἱ φίλοι εἰσίν ἀγαθοί, "the friends are good" (ἀγαθοί is the masculine plural of ἀγαθός).
Check
What is the movable nu, and when does it appear?
An optional -ν added to 3rd-person verb forms (λύουσι → λύουσιν), especially before a vowel or at the end of a sentence. It is purely euphonic — it cushions vowel clashes — and never changes the meaning or parsing.

Summary

The -ω present is stem plus six endings (-ω, -εις, -ει, -ομεν, -ετε, -ουσι(ν)) with recessive accent, covering "I loose / am loosing / do loose." The movable nu is optional euphony on third-person forms. εἰμί (εἰμί, εἶ, ἐστί(ν), ἐσμέν, ἐστέ, εἰσί(ν)) is irregular and must be memorized whole — and it is the verb you'll meet most.

Prepositions govern cases

Greek prepositions don't just sit next to nouns — they demand a case, and the case shades the meaning. The three most useful:

  • ἐν + dative — "in, among": ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ, "in the house."
  • εἰς + accusative — "into, to" (motion toward): εἰς τὸν οἶκον, "into the house."
  • ἐκ + genitive — "out of, from" (motion away): ἐκ τοῦ οἴκου, "out of the house."

One noun, three prepositions, three cases, three directions — this is the case system earning its keep. Note the pattern: rest takes the dative, motion toward the accusative, motion away the genitive. It holds across the language.

Put them in sentences

Everything here uses only lessons 1–5. Translate, then check:

  • ὁ φίλος βλέπει τὸν οἶκον. — "The friend sees the house."
  • οἱ ἄνθρωποι λέγουσιν. — "The people speak."
  • ἔχομεν εἰρήνην. — "We have peace."
  • ὁ βασιλεὺς γράφει νόμον. — "The king writes a law." (βασιλεύς is third declension — the gloss carries the form; the -ς nominative ending is your clue.)
  • ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ ἐσμὲν ἀγαθοί. — "In the house we are good."

A line from Paul

Paul's first letter to the Corinthians (13:4) opens its famous passage on love with a three-word sentence:

ἡ ἀγάπη μακροθυμεῖ.
hē agapē makrothymei.
"Love is patient."

ἡ ἀγάπη is nominative feminine — the subject. μακροθυμεῖ is 3rd singular present of μακροθυμέω, "I am patient" (literally "long-spirited": μακρός, long + θυμός, spirit). The verb is new; the gloss carries it. Everything else is yours.

Greek alive in English. Grammar (lesson 2) gave you the root table — phon, graph, log, phil, soph, dem, chron. Here is the next layer: whole Greek words walking around in English sentences.

  • σχολή (leisure) → school — the Greeks thought learning was what free people did with free time.
  • γυμνάσιον → gymnasium — the place of exercise (from γυμνός, naked).
  • θέατρον (viewing place) → theater · δρᾶμα (a thing done) → drama · μουσική (art of the Muses) → music.
  • χάος → chaos · κῦδος (glory) → kudos.
  • οἰκονομία (household management: οἶκος + νόμος) → economy · πολιτικά (affairs of the πόλις) → politics · αὐτοκρατία (self-rule) → autocracy.
  • τῆλε (far) + φωνή (sound, voice) → telephone · μικρός + σκοπέω (I look) → microscope · πᾶς + δῆμος (people) → pandemic.
  • φόβος (fear) → -phobia · φῶς (light) → photo- · ὕδωρ (water) → hydro- · θερμός (hot) → thermometer · ἄστρον (star) → astronomy · γῆ (earth) → geography.
Try
  1. Decode these English words from their Greek parts: telephone, microscope, autocracy, pandemic. Name the Greek word behind each half.
  2. Translate ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ, εἰς τὸν οἶκον, and ἐκ τοῦ οἴκου. Name the case each preposition governs.
  3. Compose in Greek: "The good friend sees the great gift." (Use ὁ ἀγαθὸς φίλος … τὸ μέγα δῶρον.)
Check
What two Greek words make "philosophy," and what do they literally mean?
φίλος (loved one, friend — "love") + σοφία (wisdom) = φιλοσοφία, "love of wisdom." Not "wisdom itself" — the love of it. The name is a confession of incompleteness.
Check
οὐ and μή both mean "not." Which one denies a plain statement of fact?
οὐ — it denies statements of fact ("he is not writing"). μή is for wishes, commands, and conditions ("do not write," "if he should not write"). At this stage, default to οὐ unless you're giving an order.
Check
What does γάρ never do, and why does that matter?
It never stands first in its clause — it is postpositive, always tucked after the first word ("the man, for, writes"). That matters because spotting γάρ or δέ in second position instantly tells you a new clause has begun.

Summary

Sixty words in five shelves: connectors, people, descriptors, big ideas, pointers. Prepositions govern cases — ἐν with the dative (rest), εἰς with the accusative (motion toward), ἐκ with the genitive (motion away). And Greek never left English: from school (leisure) to pandemic (all the people), the language is hiding in your own sentences.

Xenophon and the Ten Thousand

1. Xenophon, Anabasis, opening (slightly simplified). In 401 BC Xenophon marched with ten thousand Greek mercenaries into Persia. This is his first sentence:

In 401 BC, Cyrus the Younger — Persian prince and satrap of Lydia — hired an army of Greek mercenaries to seize the throne from his elder brother, Artaxerxes II. The Greeks won the battle of Cunaxa but Cyrus was killed, leaving ten thousand heavily armed Greeks stranded a thousand miles from home in the middle of the Persian Empire. Xenophon, an Athenian gentleman and student of Socrates who had come along as an observer, was elected one of the generals and led the fighting retreat to the Black Sea. The Anabasis ("the march up-country") is his memoir of that retreat — and because its Greek is clear, concrete, and military, it has been the classic first reader for beginners for two thousand years.

Δαρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος γίγνονται παῖδες δύο· πρεσβύτερος μὲν Ἀρταξέρξης, νεώτερος δὲ Κῦρος.

The grammar inside the sentence

Walk it with your own tools. Δαρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος are genitives — "of Darius and of Parysatis" — sitting first in the sentence, the way Greek puts the possessed before the verb. παῖδες is nominative plural, the subject ("sons"); παῖς is in your lesson 5 vocabulary, and you can see its plural here in the wild. γίγνονται is 3rd plural present — the -ονται ending is the middle-voice cousin of the -ουσι(ν) you know, and the gloss carries the meaning "are born."

Then the balanced pair: πρεσβύτερος μὲν Ἀρταξέρξης, νεώτερος δὲ Κῦρος — "the elder, on the one hand, Artaxerxes; the younger, on the other, Cyrus." The μὲν…δὲ frame is Greek's favorite hinge for contrast. Notice the word order freedom lesson 3 promised: the genitives lead, the verb follows, the appositions trail — and the endings make it all unambiguous.

The most-read Greek sentence in history

2. The opening line of John's Gospel — the most-read Greek sentence in history:

Ἐν ἀρχῇ ἦν ὁ λόγος, καὶ ὁ λόγος ἦν πρὸς τὸν θεόν, καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος.
  • Ἐν — in (+ dative)
  • ἀρχῇ — dative of ἀρχή, "beginning" → in the beginning
  • ἦν — "was" (a past tense of εἰμί; Greek II teaches this form)
  • ὁ λόγος — the Word
  • πρὸς τὸν θεόν — with God (πρός + accusative, "toward")
  • θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος — θεός has no article because it is the predicate: "the Word was God." The article marks the subject; its absence marks what is said about it.

Reading like a classicist

A method for every text you will ever meet: read aloud first (lesson 2's rules — sound is sense in Greek), then find the verb (its ending tells you the person and number), then find the subject (the nominative, often announced by the article), then hang the remaining cases on the verb — accusatives as objects, genitives as "of," datives as "to/for." Use the gloss for the words you don't know and never for the grammar you do. That is the whole discipline: sound, verb, subject, cases, gloss last.

Try
  1. Read both passages aloud using lesson 2's rules — breathings, accents, diphthongs. Then cover the glosses and translate the Xenophon sentence yourself before checking.
  2. Identify every genitive in the Xenophon sentence and say what each one depends on ("of Darius" depends on the birth being to them — whose sons?).
  3. Parse πρεσβύτερος μὲν Ἀρταξέρξης, νεώτερος δὲ Κῦρος word by word: which words are the μὲν…δὲ pair balancing?
Check
Translate the opening line of John's Gospel into English.
"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." Word by word: Ἐν ἀρχῇ ("in the beginning") ἦν ("was") ὁ λόγος ("the Word"), καὶ ("and") ὁ λόγος ἦν ("the Word was") πρὸς τὸν θεόν ("with God"), καὶ ("and") θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος ("the Word was God" — θεός without the article is the predicate).
Check
Who are the "two sons" in Xenophon's sentence, and whose sons are they?
Artaxerxes (the elder, πρεσβύτερος) and Cyrus (the younger, νεώτερος) — sons of Darius II and Parysatis. The genitives Δαρείου καὶ Παρυσάτιδος ("of Darius and of Parysatis") name the parents; παῖδες δύο ("two sons") is the subject.
Check
In καὶ θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος, why does θεός have no article?
Because it is the predicate nominative, not the subject — it says what the Word is, and Greek marks the predicate by omitting the article. ὁ λόγος keeps its article because it is the subject. Article present = the thing talked about; article absent = what is said about it.
