Twelve centuries

The classical world from the first Olympics to the closing of Plato's Academy — the stage on which everything you study here happened.

776 BC

The first Olympic Games

Tradition dates the first Olympiad here. The Greeks begin counting time in four-year Olympiads — and gymnastics becomes a civic institution.

c. 750 BC

Homer's Iliad and Odyssey

The epics take written shape. For a thousand years they are the Greek schoolbook: every educated child learns to read on Homer.

c. 700 BC

Hesiod's Theogony

The gods get a family tree — and ordinary farming life gets its poem in the Works and Days.

c. 585 BC

Thales of Miletus

Thales predicts a solar eclipse and argues water is the first principle. Philosophy — explaining the world without gods — begins.

c. 530 BC

Pythagoras

In Croton, Pythagoras founds a brotherhood where number is the essence of reality. Music and arithmetic fuse: harmony is ratio.

c. 500 BC

Heraclitus and Parmenides

Heraclitus: everything flows. Parmenides: change is illusion, argued in strict verse. Logic is born from their quarrel.

594 BC

Solon's reforms

Solon cancels debts, frees debt-slaves, and gives Athens laws it can read. The idea that law binds rulers too takes root.

508 BC

Democracy in Athens

Cleisthenes reorganizes Athens around ten tribes and the assembly. Ordinary citizens will now need rhetoric — persuasion becomes a civic art.

490 BC

Marathon

Athens defeats Persia against the odds. The victory funds a cultural explosion — and the legend of the long run.

480 BC

Thermopylae and Salamis

Leonidas holds the pass; Themistocles wins at sea. Greece survives the Persian Empire.

c. 470–399 BC

Socrates

He writes nothing, questions everything, and teaches Athens to examine itself — dialectic as a way of life.

447–432 BC

The Parthenon

Pericles rebuilds Athens in marble. The Parthenon's proportions are geometry made civic.

441 BC

Sophocles' Antigone

A girl buries her brother against the king's edict: the first great dramatization of conscience versus law.

431–404 BC

The Peloponnesian War

Athens and Sparta tear Greece apart. Thucydides writes its history — the first work of political realism.

399 BC

Socrates executed

Athens condemns its gadfly for corrupting the youth. Plato never forgives the democracy — and writes the Apology.

387 BC

Plato founds the Academy

The first university: mathematics required at the door ("let no one ignorant of geometry enter"). It will run for 900 years.

c. 380 BC

Plato's Republic

Justice in the soul, justice in the city — and the allegory of the cave, still the sharpest image of education ever drawn.

335 BC

Aristotle founds the Lyceum

Plato's student builds the rival school: logic systematized, biology observed, ethics grounded in habit. The Organon rules reasoning for two millennia.

336–323 BC

Alexander's conquests

In thirteen years Alexander carries Greek language and cities from Egypt to India. The Hellenistic world — Greek culture without the polis — begins.

c. 300 BC

Euclid's Elements

In Alexandria, Euclid assembles the thirteen books of geometry — the most studied textbook in history.

c. 300 BC

Zeno's Stoa

Zeno teaches in the Painted Porch at Athens. Stoicism is born: virtue as the only good, the cosmos as one city.

306 BC

Epicurus' Garden

Epicurus buys a house and teaches that pleasure — understood as the absence of pain — is the goal. The great rival of the Stoa.

c. 280 BC

The Library of Alexandria

The Ptolemies gather the world's books — perhaps half a million scrolls. Scholarship becomes an institution.

c. 250 BC

Archimedes

In Syracuse, Archimedes computes pi, invents the science of levers and buoyancy, and nearly invents calculus.

c. 150 BC

Hipparchus

The greatest Greek astronomer: star catalog, precession of the equinoxes, trigonometry for the heavens.

146 BC

Rome conquers Greece

Corinth falls. As Horace will put it: captive Greece takes her savage captor captive — Roman elites now learn Greek.

44 BC

Caesar assassinated

The Ides of March. Cicero, the republic's voice, writes his philosophical works in the wreckage — and is proscribed for it.

27 BC

Augustus and the Pax Romana

Octavian becomes Augustus. Two centuries of relative peace let law, roads, and Latin literature flourish — Virgil, Horace, Ovid.

c. 50 AD

Epictetus and the Enchiridion

A freed slave teaches Stoicism at Nicopolis; his student Arrian writes it down. The handbook of inner freedom.

79 AD

Vesuvius buries Pompeii

A Roman city frozen mid-day: graffiti, bread in ovens, inscriptions — Latin as people actually wrote it.

117 AD

The empire at its height

Under Trajan, Rome rules from Britain to Mesopotamia. One law, one coinage, two literary languages.

161–180 AD

Marcus Aurelius' Meditations

On campaign against the Marcomanni, the emperor writes his private notebook in Greek: Stoicism as a daily practice. Never meant for us — which is why it works.

212 AD

Universal citizenship

Caracalla extends Roman citizenship to every free man in the empire. Law becomes the empire's true language.

235–284 AD

Crisis of the Third Century

Fifty years of civil war, plague, and invasion nearly end the empire. Diocletian rebuilds it as an autocracy.

313 AD

Edict of Milan

Constantine legalizes Christianity. The empire's spiritual center of gravity begins to move.

325 AD

Council of Nicaea

Bishops define orthodoxy by vote — rhetoric and logic turned to theology.

410 AD

The sack of Rome

Alaric's Goths take the city. Jerome writes: "the city which had taken the whole world was itself taken."

476 AD

The Western Empire ends

The traditional date: the last western emperor deposed. In the east, Rome continues another thousand years.

529 AD

The Academy closed

Justinian shuts Plato's Academy after more than nine centuries. Antiquity ends; the texts go into monasteries — and wait for you.
