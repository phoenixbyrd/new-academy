The Library

Read the ancients themselves

Commentary is a map; the text is the territory. Everything taught in the courses was drawn from these books — read them slowly, firsthand, and argue with them.

Meditations

Marcus Aurelius, Emperor of Rome · c. 170–180 CE · trans. George Long (1862)

Why read it — A Roman emperor's private notebook, written in Greek on campaign along the Danube and never meant for publication. This is Stoicism as a daily practice rather than a theory: short reminders a powerful man wrote to keep himself honest.

Key ideas

  • Control what is yours: your judgments, impulses, and desires. Everything else — reputation, outcomes, other people — is not.
  • Strip events of the stories you add to them. "Life is opinion": most suffering is judgment, not fact.
  • Expect friction. Begin each day rehearsing the difficult people you will meet, so they cannot hijack your mind.
  • Remember what you are — "a little flesh and breath, and the ruling part" — and act like it while there is time.
"Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial."— George Long's translation, Book II
"The universe is transformation: life is opinion."— George Long's translation, Book IV
"Whatever this is that I am, it is a little flesh and breath, and the ruling part."— George Long's translation, Book II

Enchiridion

Epictetus (compiled by Arrian) · c. 100 CE · trans. Thomas Wentworth Higginson

Why read it — The shortest complete Stoic manual: 53 chapters you can read in an hour and practice for a lifetime. A freed slave's lectures, distilled by his student into rules for inner freedom.

Key ideas

  • Some things are up to us — opinion, aim, desire, aversion — and everything else is not. Freedom starts with sorting the two.
  • We are disturbed not by things but by our views of things; the work is on the judgment, not the world.
  • Do not demand that events match your wishes; wish events to be as they are.
  • Measure progress by blame: the beginner blames himself, the instructed blame no one — not others, not themselves.
"There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power. Within our power are opinion, aim, desire, aversion, and, in one word, whatever affairs are our own."— trans. Thomas Wentworth Higginson, ch. 1
"Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things."— trans. Thomas Wentworth Higginson, ch. 5
"Demand not that events should happen as you wish; but wish them to happen as they do happen, and you will go on well."— trans. Thomas Wentworth Higginson, ch. 8

Apology

Plato · 399 BCE (dramatic date) · trans. Benjamin Jowett (1871)

Why read it — Socrates' defense speech at his trial for corrupting the youth. The founding document of the examined life — and a masterclass in cross-examination under mortal pressure.

Key ideas

  • Socratic ignorance: wisdom begins in knowing that you do not know what you do not know.
  • The examined life is the only one worth living; comfortable unexamined existence is a kind of death.
  • The elenchus: test claims by questioning them until contradictions surface or they survive.
  • Integrity over survival: Socrates will not stop philosophizing even to save his own life.
"The unexamined life is not worth living."— trans. Benjamin Jowett, 38a
"I neither know nor think that I know."— trans. Benjamin Jowett, 21d
"The hour of departure has arrived, and we go our ways — I to die, and you to live. Which is better God only knows."— trans. Benjamin Jowett, 42a

Nicomachean Ethics (selections)

Aristotle · c. 350 BCE · trans. W. D. Ross

Why read it — The most influential account of what a good human life is: happiness as activity rather than feeling, virtue as habit, excellence as the mean between extremes. Read Books I and II first; the rest rewards patience.

Key ideas

  • Every action aims at some good; the highest good — happiness — is activity of the soul in accord with virtue.
  • Virtue is a settled disposition to choose the mean between excess and deficiency, as practical wisdom determines it.
  • Character is built by habituation: we become just by doing just acts, not by studying justice.
  • Ethics is a skill, not a formula — the mean is "relative to us," found by the person of practical wisdom.
"Every action and pursuit, is thought to aim at some good; and for this reason the good has rightly been declared to be that at which all things aim."— trans. W. D. Ross, Book I
"We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts."— trans. W. D. Ross, Book II
"Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it."— trans. W. D. Ross, Book II

Euclid's Elements, Book I (selections)

Euclid · c. 300 BCE · ed. John Casey (1885)

Why read it — The model of deductive reasoning for two thousand years. From a handful of definitions and axioms, Book I builds 48 propositions — ending in the Pythagorean theorem — with every step proved. This is what "rigor" meant before the word existed.

Key ideas

  • Begin from agreed definitions and self-evident axioms; prove everything else. No hand-waving allowed.
  • Axioms need no proof — "things equal to the same thing are equal to one another" is the ground proof stands on.
  • Proof is construction: Euclid builds figures with straightedge and compass, he does not merely argue about them.
  • Book I culminates in Proposition 47, the Pythagorean theorem — a summit reached one proved step at a time.
"A point is that which has position but not dimensions."— John Casey's edition, Definition I
"Things which are equal to the same thing are equal to one another."— John Casey's edition, Axiom I
"The whole is greater than its part."— John Casey's edition, Axiom V

Antigone

Sophocles · c. 441 BCE · trans. R. C. Jebb

Why read it — The tragedy of two rights in collision: Antigone buries her brother in obedience to the unwritten laws of the gods; Creon forbids it by decree of the state. No villain, no easy answer — the form every hard moral conflict has taken since.

Key ideas

  • Divine law versus human law: conscience can command what the state forbids, and the play refuses to dissolve the tension.
  • Creon's rigidity destroys what it tries to protect — stubbornness presented as a political vice, not a virtue.
  • The "Ode to Man": human ingenuity is wonderful and terrible at once — the chorus marvels and warns.
  • Wisdom is admitting error and repairing it; no one man alone is wise.
"Wonders are many, and none is more wonderful than man."— trans. R. C. Jebb, chorus
"'Tis not my nature to join in hating, but in loving."— trans. R. C. Jebb, Antigone
"Wear not, then, one mood only in thyself; think not that thy word, and thine alone, must be right. For if any man thinks that he alone is wise, — that in speech, or in mind, he hath no peer, — such a soul, when laid open, is ever found empty."— trans. R. C. Jebb, Haemon

Parallel Lives (selections)

Plutarch · c. 100 CE · trans. Aubrey Stewart and George Long

Why read it — Biography as moral education. Plutarch pairs Greek and Roman lives — Theseus with Romulus, Solon with Poplicola — to show character revealed in small moments, not just on battlefields. The template for every biography since.

Key ideas

  • Character over achievement: a phrase or a jest can reveal more than a campaign.
  • Fortune is fickle: judge no life happy until it is finished — Solon's warning to Croesus.
  • Written law is a spider's web: it catches the weak while the strong break through.
  • Speak the necessary truth even to power that can punish you: "Strike, but hear me."
"Strange things await every man in the unknown future; and we think that man alone happy whose life has been brought to a fortunate termination."— trans. Stewart and Long, Life of Solon
"Anacharsis laughed at Solon's undertaking, if he thought to restrain the crimes and greed of the citizens by written laws, which he said were just like spiders' webs; for, like them, they caught the weaker criminals, but were broken through by the stronger and more important."— trans. Stewart and Long, Life of Solon
"'True,' said Themistokles, 'but they do not crown those who lag behind.'" — trans. Stewart and Long, Life of Themistokles

On Duties (selections)

Cicero · 44 BCE · trans. Walter Miller (1913)

Why read it — Rome's handbook for public life, written in the last year of Cicero's life for his son. How the honorable and the useful fit together — and why they never truly conflict. The Stoic ethics of the Meditations, translated into citizenship.

Key ideas

  • We are not born for ourselves alone: our country and our friends claim a share of us.
  • Justice rests on good faith — truth and fidelity to promises, kept even when costly.
  • True glory is the shadow of duty discharged, never the prize of chasing applause.
  • The honorable and the expedient only appear to conflict; apparent conflicts are errors in judgment.
"But since, as Plato has admirably expressed it, we are not born for ourselves alone, but our country claims a share of our being, and our friends a share."— trans. Walter Miller, Book I
"The foundation of justice, moreover, is good faith — that is, truth and fidelity to promises and agreements."— trans. Walter Miller, Book I
"If, therefore, anyone wishes to win true glory, let him discharge the duties required by justice."— trans. Walter Miller, Book II

On the Nature of Things (selections)

Lucretius · c. 55 BCE · trans. William Ellery Leonard (1916)

Why read it — An Epicurean poem with the boldest thesis of antiquity: the universe is atoms and void, the gods don't intervene, and death is nothing to fear. Physics in service of peace of mind.

Key ideas

  • Nothing comes from nothing: nature runs on cause, not miracle or divine whim.
  • Death is nothing to us — non-existence after death mirrors non-existence before birth, which never harmed us.
  • Religion's terrors are dispelled by understanding nature, not by ritual or prayer.
  • Philosophy may be sweetened: honey on the cup's rim helps the bitter medicine go down.
"Nothing from nothing ever yet was born."— trans. William Ellery Leonard, Book I
"Look back: Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld / Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth. / And Nature holds this like a mirror up / Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone."— trans. William Ellery Leonard, Book III
"But as physicians, when they seek to give / Young boys the nauseous wormwood, first do touch / The brim around the cup with the sweet juice / And yellow of the honey, in order that / The thoughtless age of boyhood be cajoled / As far as the lips, and meanwhile swallow down / The wormwood's bitter draught."— trans. William Ellery Leonard, Book I

Histories (selections)

Herodotus · c. 440 BCE · trans. G. C. Macaulay (1890)

Why read it — The first history in Western literature: an inquiry into why Greeks and barbarians went to war, told through stories, customs, marvels, and digressions. The inventor of "let me look into that."

Key ideas

  • History is inquiry (historiē): preserve great deeds from oblivion and investigate their causes.
  • Custom is king: every people holds its own customs best — understanding others means stepping outside your own.
  • Power overreaches: the great kings' expansions carry the seeds of their defeats.
  • Prefer eyes to ears: check stories against evidence rather than merely collecting them.
"This is the Showing forth of the Inquiry of Herodotus of Halicarnassos, to the end that neither the deeds of men may be forgotten by lapse of time, nor the works great and marvellous, which have been produced some by Hellenes and some by Barbarians, may lose their renown."— trans. G. C. Macaulay, Book I
"Gyges, I think that thou dost not believe me when I tell thee of the beauty of my wife, for it happens that men's ears are less apt of belief than their eyes: contrive therefore means by which thou mayest look upon her naked."— trans. G. C. Macaulay, Book I (Candaules to Gyges)
"I think that Pindar spoke rightly in his verse, when he said that 'of all things law is king.'" — trans. G. C. Macaulay, Book III (on custom)

How to read

  • Read slowly. Ten pages with a pencil beat a hundred pages at speed. These books were written to be lived with, not finished.
  • Keep a commonplace book. Copy out the sentences that stop you, in your own hand, with the page number. Your future self will thank you.
  • Argue with the author. Assent is cheap. Ask what would prove them wrong, steelman their case, then decide what you actually believe.
